## Overview

Goryōkaku is a five-pointed star fort in Hakodate, on Japan's northern island of Hokkaido. Its name means "five-point fort". It was designed in a European style by the Japanese scholar Takeda Ayasaburō, at a time when Japan had only just opened up to the West. Today it is a park famous for cherry blossoms, with a reconstructed magistrate's office at its centre.

## The main story

- **1850s** – After Hakodate is opened as a port to foreign ships, the Tokugawa shogunate orders Takeda Ayasaburō to design a fortress to protect the area. He bases it on European star fort designs.
- **c. 1857–1866** – The fort is built. Sources give completion as 1864 or 1866.
- **1868–1869** – During the Boshin War, forces loyal to the old shogunate under Enomoto Takeaki take the fort and declare the short-lived Republic of Ezo. Imperial forces besiege it, and it surrenders in 1869.
- **20th century** – The site becomes a public park.
- **2010** – The Hakodate magistrate's office in the centre of the fort is reconstructed.

## The alternative view

### A European star in Japan

A star fort with geometry matching forts across Europe and the Americas appears in northern Japan in the 1850s, designed from books by a scholar who had never built one. The alternative view asks whether star forts are evidence of a shared worldwide design tradition, and whether Goryōkaku really started from nothing.

### Built for a war it barely fought

The fort was finished in the 1860s and saw one short siege before becoming a park. Was it really needed as a fortress, or did its shape serve another purpose?

### Geometry seen from above

Like Palmanova, its shape is only fully visible from the air or from the modern observation tower beside it. Why use such precise geometry?

### What would support the alternative view

- Earthworks or structures older than the 1850s on the site
- Gaps or inconsistencies in the construction records
- Features of the layout with no clear military use

### What would weaken it

- Takeda Ayasaburō's plans and the shogunate's building records
- Contemporary drawings and photographs of the construction
- Archaeological findings from the 2010 reconstruction
