## Overview

Manchester Town Hall fills a triangular site on Albert Square in the centre of the city. Its main front, with a clock tower about 85 m (280 ft) high, faces the square, and the building wraps around inner courtyards with a Great Hall at the centre. The Great Hall is decorated with twelve murals by Ford Madox Brown telling the history of Manchester.

It was designed by Alfred Waterhouse, who later designed the [Natural History Museum](../natural-history-museum/natural-history-museum.md) in London, after a competition with 137 entries. The foundation stone was laid on 26 October 1868, and the building was opened on 13 September 1877 by the mayor, Abel Heywood, after Queen Victoria declined to come. It replaced Manchester's first town hall on King Street, which was later demolished; its columns were re-erected as a ruin-like colonnade in Heaton Park.

## The main story

By the 1860s, Manchester had outgrown its first town hall on King Street, and the city wanted a building to match its wealth as the centre of the cotton trade.

- **1822–1825** – Manchester's first town hall is built on King Street, at the corner of Cross Street, to a neoclassical design by Francis Goodwin. (One source gives 1819–34.)
- **1867–1868** – A design competition for a new town hall on a triangular site draws 137 entries. Waterhouse's design is said to have been placed only fourth for its looks, but first for its planning and use of light.
- **1 April 1868** – Waterhouse is appointed architect.
- **26 October 1868** – The mayor, Robert Neill, lays the foundation stone.
- **1868–1877** – The building goes up over about nine years, using more than 14 million bricks faced in Spinkwell stone, a hard sandstone from Bradford Dale in Yorkshire, chosen to stand up to Manchester's polluted air. Waterhouse uses one stone throughout rather than mixing colours.
- **13 September 1877** – Abel Heywood, the mayor who had championed the project, opens the Town Hall. Queen Victoria has declined to attend. The great bell in the clock tower is named "Great Abel" after him. A commemorative album of photographs of the finished building, by J. McLeod with text by Waterhouse, is published for the opening.
- **Cost** – Estimates range from about £775,000 to about £1,000,000.
- **1879–1893** – Ford Madox Brown paints the twelve Manchester Murals in the Great Hall. Six were originally to be painted by Frederic Shields, who withdrew. Brown finishes the last shortly before his death in 1893.
- **1912** – The old town hall on King Street, later used as a library and a bank, is demolished. After a campaign led by the architect Edgar Wood, its colonnade is saved and re-erected by the lake in Heaton Park.
- **1934–1938** – The Town Hall Extension, by E. Vincent Harris, is built next to it, linked by bridges.
- **October 2018** – The Town Hall closes for a major restoration, the "Our Town Hall" project. Waterhouse's original drawings are found in a corner of the basement during the work. The budget rises from about £330 million to over £400 million, and reopening moves from 2024 to spring 2027.

## Conservation

- The Town Hall is **Grade I listed**.
- The Our Town Hall project is renewing its electrics, plumbing, heating, ventilation and lifts, as well as repairing the stonework and interiors.
- The old town hall's colonnade in Heaton Park is listed Grade II*.

## The alternative view

The official account is of a confident Victorian city building itself a Gothic palace of local government. Looked at from an "old world" perspective, several things invite a second look: an earlier town hall demolished and its front moved to a park, an architect's plans found in the basement more than 140 years later, a nine-year build with no construction photographs found so far, and a medieval style chosen for a modern council. Each point below carries an evidence label: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative** (see the [research brief](https://github.com/martinv678/our-old-world/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

### The old town hall moved to a park

**Documented** – Manchester's first town hall on King Street was only about 50 years old when it was replaced, and it was demolished in 1912. Its columned front was taken down and re-erected beside the lake in Heaton Park, where it stands as a free-standing colonnade, like a classical ruin. This is the same pattern as [Temple Bar](../royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md#temple-bar-a-gate-taken-down-next-door) in London and the [Crystal Palace](../crystal-palace/crystal-palace.md): a building taken apart and rebuilt elsewhere. The colonnade in Heaton Park is worth examining: how much of it is original stone, and how closely does it match photographs of the King Street building?

### Plans found in the basement

**Documented** – During the restoration that began in 2018, Waterhouse's original drawings were found "stuffed in a corner of the basement". This research brief lists "plans that are lost, rediscovered at the right moment" among the things to look for. Here the drawings seem to have simply been forgotten in a large building, and their discovery is useful for the restoration (**Speculative** as to anything more). The important question for this project is what they show: do they match the building as built, and do they include construction drawings, dated revisions and site plans?

### No construction photographs found

**Observed** (none found so far) – As with the [Royal Courts of Justice](../royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md#no-construction-photographs-found), searches have not yet found any photograph of Manchester Town Hall under construction, despite a nine-year build in the middle of a large city. What does exist is a commemorative album of photographs of the finished building, taken by J. McLeod for the opening in 1877 and now in the Historic England Archive.

This makes two Gothic public buildings of exactly the same years (1868–77 and 1873–82) with no known construction photographs, while the [Royal Albert Hall](../royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md) (1867–71) and [Tower Bridge](../tower-bridge/tower-bridge.md) (1886–94) were well photographed. For the alternative view, this is worth pursuing: if these buildings were not photographed while being built, why not (**Speculative**)?

Points to weigh up:

- **Progress photography wasn't routine yet.** The Albert Hall was a national project linked to the royal family and the South Kensington museums, which had their own photographers. A city council may not have commissioned progress photographs.
- **Engravings were the normal record.** *The Builder*, *The Building News* and the local press may have published views of the works in progress.
- **Local archives haven't been searched.** Manchester Archives+, the Manchester Local Image Collection and the newly found Waterhouse drawings are the places to look.

**How to test it:** search Manchester Archives+ and the Manchester Local Image Collection for images dated 1868–77, and the volumes of *The Builder* and *The Building News* for engravings of the works.

### A medieval palace for a modern council

**Documented** (style) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – Waterhouse designed the Town Hall in the Gothic style of 13th-century Europe, with a great tower, vaulted corridors and a hall like a medieval guildhall. It belongs to the same wave of Gothic public buildings as the [Palace of Westminster](../palace-of-westminster/palace-of-westminster.md), the [Royal Courts of Justice](../royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md) and Vienna's City Hall (1872–83), and was built in the same years that [Cologne Cathedral](../../germany/cologne-cathedral/cologne-cathedral.md) was finally completed. In the "shared worldwide architecture" theme, the question is why cities across Europe chose the same medieval style for new government buildings at the same time. The ordinary answer is the Gothic Revival, a well-documented movement, and Manchester is often said to have wanted to compare itself with the free trading cities of medieval Flanders and Germany.

### The Great Hall's version of history

**Documented** (murals) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – The twelve Ford Madox Brown murals present an official story of Manchester, from the Romans building a fort at Mamucium to John Dalton's science and the industries that made the city rich. They were painted between 1879 and 1893, after the building was finished. For this project, they're an example of a Victorian institution setting out its own history on its walls, like the inscription on the [Royal Albert Hall](../royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md#the-history-written-in-stone). They're worth reading closely for which events are included and which are left out.

### The triangular site and the ground level

**Speculative** – The building fills an awkward triangular site, and the brief's research methods ask what stood there before. No evidence of buried floors has been found. Old maps of the area before 1868 would show what was cleared, and the restoration works, which have opened up the basement, may show what lies below.

### Dates and inscriptions

**Speculative** – The foundation stone of 26 October 1868, and any dated stones or inscriptions on the building, haven't been checked yet for the [letters in dates](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#letters-in-dates-the-j-argument) argument.

### Timeline check

**What we know:** the foundation stone was laid on 26 October 1868 and the building opened on 13 September 1877, about 8 years and 11 months later. It used more than 14 million bricks, faced in Spinkwell stone, with a clock tower about 85 m high.

**The working:**

- **Brickwork:** 14 million bricks over about 7 years of wall-building (about 360 working weeks) is about 39,000 bricks a week, or about 6,500 a day over a six-day week. At the commonly quoted Victorian rate of 500–1,000 bricks per bricklayer per day (not confirmed here), that's about 7 to 13 bricklayers working at once. That's easily achievable.
- **Stone:** the Spinkwell stone facing and carved work would have been the slower part, cut at the quarry and on site.
- **Comparison:** the [Royal Courts of Justice](../royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md), a stone building of similar size, took about nine years. The [Natural History Museum](../natural-history-museum/natural-history-museum.md), by the same architect, took about eight.

**Working still needed:**

1. The workforce and the main contractors.
2. The volume of stone and where each part was cut.
3. The order in which the parts of the building were completed.

**Verdict for now:** **Plausible.** Nine years is a normal timeline for a large stone-faced building of this period. As with the Royal Courts of Justice, the timeline isn't the question; the stronger points are the missing construction photographs, the drawings found in the basement and the old town hall moved to Heaton Park.

### What would support the alternative view

- A thorough search of local and national archives that finds no photograph or engraving of the works in 1868–77
- Waterhouse's rediscovered drawings showing a building different from what was built, or no construction drawings at all
- Older structures found on the site in the restoration works
- Differences between the Heaton Park colonnade and the King Street building as photographed

### What would weaken it

- Construction photographs or engravings of the works, in Manchester's archives or in *The Builder*
- The rediscovered Waterhouse drawings, with dated revisions and site plans
- The council's minutes and the building committee's records, 1867–77
- Contemporary newspaper reports of the progress of the work

## Open questions for contributors

- **Construction images:** Does any photograph or engraving of the Town Hall under construction exist?
- **The drawings:** What exactly was found in the basement in 2018, and where are the drawings now?
- **Cost:** What did the building cost?
- **Contractors and workforce:** Who built it, and how many people worked on it?
- **The colonnade:** How much of the Heaton Park colonnade is original?
- **The site:** What stood on the triangular site before 1868?
- **Dates:** Where is the foundation stone, and how is its date written?

- **Manchester Archives+ and the Manchester Local Image Collection:** the council's records, the building committee minutes and any images of the works.
