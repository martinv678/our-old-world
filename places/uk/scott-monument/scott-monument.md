## Overview

The Scott Monument is a Gothic spire about 61 m (200 ft 6 in) high, standing in Princes Street Gardens in the centre of Edinburgh. It was built in memory of the novelist Sir Walter Scott, who died in 1832. Under its open arches sits a marble statue of Scott with his dog Maida, and its pinnacles and niches hold dozens of carved figures of characters from his novels. Visitors can climb 287 steps to the top.

Its designer was George Meikle Kemp, a self-taught carpenter and draughtsman with no formal training as an architect, who entered the competition under the name of a medieval mason. He drowned in the Union Canal in March 1844, months before the spire was finished. Unusually for this project, the monument was photographed while it was being built: the early photographers David Octavius Hill and Robert Adamson recorded it in 1843–44, among the first construction photographs ever taken.

## The main story

After Scott's death in 1832, Edinburgh set out to build the grandest monument ever raised to a writer.

- **21 September 1832** – Sir Walter Scott dies.
- **1836–1838** – A design competition is held. George Meikle Kemp, a joiner and draughtsman who had spent years studying and drawing Gothic buildings, especially Melrose Abbey, enters under the name "John Morvo", after a medieval master mason of Melrose Abbey. His design is first placed third, behind Thomas Rickman and Charles Fowler; after revisions it is chosen, and the result is announced in 1838.
- **15 August 1840** – The foundation stone is laid on the anniversary of Scott's birth, in the gardens made from the drained Nor' Loch below Princes Street. The foundations are said to go about 52 ft (16 m) deep (**Reported**).
- **1840–1844** – The spire is built of Binny sandstone from West Lothian.
- **1843–1844** – The photographers David Octavius Hill and Robert Adamson photograph the monument at almost every stage of its construction, from Calton Hill and the roof of the Royal Institution.
- **6 March 1844** – Kemp drowns in the Union Canal on a foggy evening, before the monument is finished. William Bonnar, a relative of Kemp by marriage, oversees the rest of the work.
- **26 October 1844** – The monument is topped out; Kemp's son Thomas places the final stone. William Henry Fox Talbot photographs it near completion for his book *Sun Pictures in Scotland*.
- **15 August 1846** – The monument is inaugurated, with John Steell's statue of Scott, carved from a single block of Carrara marble, in place. The total cost is a little over £16,000.
- **Later 19th century** – Figures of characters from Scott's novels are added to the niches over the following decades (sources give 64 or 68 figures).
- **1990s** – A major restoration, costing about £2.36 million, repairs the stonework. The closed Binny quarry is reopened to supply matching stone, and the new stone stands out pale against the smoke-blackened original.

## Conservation

- The Scott Monument is **Category A listed**, with its retaining wall and steps.
- It's owned and run by the City of Edinburgh Council and is open to visitors.
- The 1990s restoration was funded by the Heritage Lottery Fund, Historic Scotland and the City of Edinburgh Council.

## The alternative view

The official account is of a Victorian memorial designed by a self-taught genius and built in four years. Looked at from an "old world" perspective, several things invite a second look: a designer with no training who used the name of a medieval mason, his death by drowning before the work was finished, a 200 ft stone spire on the bed of a drained loch, and a style copied from a medieval abbey. But it also has something most of the buildings in this project lack: photographs of it being built. Each point below carries an evidence label: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative** (see the [research brief](https://github.com/martinv678/our-old-world/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

### Photographed while it was built

**Documented** – Hill and Adamson, two of the first great photographers, used the new calotype process to photograph the monument at almost every stage between 1843 and its completion in 1844, and Fox Talbot photographed it near completion in October 1844. Their photographs show the spire part-built, with scaffolding at its base and around its pinnacles. They are held by the National Galleries of Scotland, the University of Glasgow and the University of St Andrews.

This is important for the [photo record argument](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#missing-construction-photographs-the-photo-record-argument). The Scott Monument is a **Gothic** structure, made to look like a medieval abbey, and it was photographed while being built, in the earliest years of photography, more than 20 years before the Royal Courts of Justice and Manchester Town Hall. So a medieval style on its own is no barrier to a construction record. It joins [Tower Bridge](../tower-bridge/tower-bridge.md#a-benchmark-for-construction-photographs) as a benchmark: this is what a building looks like when it really was photographed going up.

### A self-taught designer with a medieval mason's name

**Documented** (pseudonym) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – Kemp had no formal architectural training. He was a joiner who taught himself by walking around Scotland and England drawing Gothic buildings, above all Melrose Abbey. He entered the competition as "John Morvo", the name of a medieval master mason associated with Melrose Abbey. For the [skill and precision](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#skill-and-precision-the-master-builders-argument) argument, a 200 ft Gothic spire designed by someone without formal training is remarkable, though the detailed construction depended on the masons and engineers who carried it out. The use of a medieval master's name is a striking detail: did Kemp see himself as continuing the work of the old masons?

### The designer who drowned

**Documented** (death) / **Speculative** (pattern) – Kemp drowned in the Union Canal on a foggy evening on 6 March 1844, seven months before the spire was topped out. He is one of several designers in this project who died before their buildings were finished, and one of only two whose deaths were sudden accidents:

- Harvey Lonsdale Elmes: [St George's Hall](../st-georges-hall-liverpool/st-georges-hall-liverpool.md) (died 1847)
- George Meikle Kemp: Scott Monument (drowned 1844)
- Captain Francis Fowke: [Royal Albert Hall](../royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md) and [Natural History Museum](../natural-history-museum/natural-history-museum.md) (died 1865)
- Giuseppe Mengoni: [Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II](../../italy/galleria-vittorio-emanuele-ii/galleria-vittorio-emanuele-ii.md) (fell from scaffolding, 1877)
- George Edmund Street: [Royal Courts of Justice](../royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md) (died 1881)
- Horace Jones: [Tower Bridge](../tower-bridge/tower-bridge.md) (died 1887)

The circumstances of Kemp's death, and whether an inquest was held, are worth checking in the Edinburgh newspapers of March 1844.

### A spire on the bed of a drained loch

**Documented** (site) / **Reported** (depth) / **Speculative** (mud flood) – Princes Street Gardens were made from the Nor' Loch, a lake below the Old Town that had become a dumping ground and was drained between about 1813 and 1820. The monument stands on this former loch bed, and its foundations are said to go about 52 ft deep to reach firm ground. In mud flood research, made-up ground, filled valleys and deep foundations are of interest. Here the history of the loch and its draining is well recorded, and the deep foundations are what engineers would expect on soft, filled ground.

**How to test it:** find the engineers' reports on the foundations, and any record of what was found when the ground was dug out in 1840.

### Copying a medieval abbey

**Documented** (source) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – Kemp based the design on Melrose Abbey and other Scottish Gothic buildings he had drawn. In the "shared worldwide architecture" theme, it belongs to the wave of Gothic monuments of the mid-19th century, alongside the Albert Memorial in London (1864–76) and the completion of [Cologne Cathedral](../../germany/cologne-cathedral/cologne-cathedral.md). Unusually, the record here shows exactly where the design came from: Kemp's own drawings of Melrose.

### Blackened stone and the reopened quarry

**Documented** – The Binny sandstone has blackened badly, partly from Edinburgh's coal smoke ("Auld Reekie") and partly, it is often said, because the stone contains oil (**Reported**). For the 1990s restoration, the closed Binny quarry was reopened to supply matching stone, and the new pieces stand out pale against the old. This is a good example of how much of a 19th-century monument's surface may have been replaced in recent decades.

### Dates and inscriptions

**Speculative** – The foundation stone of 1840 and any inscriptions on the monument haven't been checked yet for the [letters in dates](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#letters-in-dates-the-j-argument) argument.

### Timeline check

**What we know:** the foundation stone was laid on 15 August 1840 and the spire was topped out on 26 October 1844, about four years. It is about 61 m high, built of Binny sandstone, and cost a little over £16,000.

**The working:**

- **Time:** four years for a 200 ft stone spire, with deep foundations on soft ground, is reasonable, and the photographs show the work progressing in 1843–44.
- **Method:** hand-cut and carved sandstone, raised with scaffolding and cranes, as the calotypes show.
- **Delivery check:** the stone came from Binny, near Ecclesmachan in West Lothian, about 12 miles west of Edinburgh. The Union Canal, opened in 1822, ran from that area into Edinburgh, so the stone may have come by canal (to be confirmed). The quantity of stone hasn't been found yet.

**Working still needed:**

1. The quantity of stone and how it was carried.
2. The size of the workforce.
3. The dates of each stage, from the dated photographs.

**Verdict for now:** **Plausible**, and unusually well recorded, with photographs of the work in progress. The interest of this entry for the project lies in what it shows by comparison: a Gothic structure of the 1840s with a construction record, set against later Gothic buildings without one.

### What would support the alternative view

- Evidence that the foundations or lower stages are older than 1840
- Contradictions between the dated photographs and the recorded stages of building
- Gaps in the records of Kemp's design, or doubt over who really designed it

### What would weaken it

- The Hill and Adamson and Fox Talbot photographs of the work in progress
- Kemp's drawings of Melrose and of the monument
- The committee's records and accounts, and reports in the Edinburgh newspapers, 1838–46
- The restoration records of the 1990s

## Open questions for contributors

- **The photographs:** Can the Hill and Adamson photographs be put in date order, to show the stages of building?
- **Kemp's death:** What happened on 6 March 1844? Was there an inquest?
- **The foundations:** How deep do they really go, and what was found when the ground was dug?
- **The stone:** How much stone was used, and did it come by the Union Canal?
- **The figures:** How many character figures are there, 64 or 68, and when was each added?
- **John Morvo:** What is known about the medieval mason whose name Kemp used?
