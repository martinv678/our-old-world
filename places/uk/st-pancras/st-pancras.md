## Overview

St Pancras is a railway station in north London with two famous parts. At the back is William Barlow's huge arched iron and glass train shed. At the front, facing Euston Road, is George Gilbert Scott's red-brick Gothic hotel, with its spires and clock tower. Beneath the platforms lies a vast undercroft, once a beer warehouse. To build it all, the railway cleared a whole neighbourhood and part of a churchyard. Today it is the London terminal for Eurostar trains to Europe.

## The main story

- **1863** – The Midland Railway decides to build its own London terminus. The land includes Agar Town, a poor neighbourhood, and part of the churchyard of St Pancras Old Church.
- **1865** – Graves in the churchyard are exhumed and moved. The architect Arthur Blomfield passes the supervision of the work to his young assistant, **Thomas Hardy**, later the novelist. Gravestones from the site are stacked around an ash tree, later known as the Hardy Tree.
- **1866** – Agar Town is cleared within a couple of months of its sale to the railway by the Church Commissioners. Its residents move to nearby districts.
- **1865–1868** – The train shed is built. Because the tracks have to cross the Regent's Canal just to the north, they are raised about 5 m above the street. Barlow fills the space underneath with an undercroft, a grid of several hundred cast-iron columns used to store beer from Burton upon Trent. The iron arch ribs are made by the Butterley Company in Derbyshire; one rib carries the plate "Manufactured by the Butterley Company, 1867". The arched roof spans about 73 m (240 ft; one source gives 245 ft 6 in), the largest single span in the world when it opens in 1868.
- **1868–1876** – Scott's Midland Grand Hotel is built in stages in front of the shed. It opens in 1873 and is completed in 1876. Scott is often said to have reused ideas from his unbuilt Gothic design for government offices in Whitehall. The clock in the tower is made by the Dent company, makers of the Great Clock of Westminster.
- **1935** – The hotel closes, having fallen out of date, and becomes railway offices, renamed St Pancras Chambers.
- **1960s** – The station and hotel are threatened with demolition. A campaign, led in part by the poet John Betjeman, saves them, and they are listed Grade I in 1967.
- **1970s** – The great clock on the platform end is taken down and smashed. A retiring railwayman, Roland Hoggard, buys the pieces for £25, rebuilds the clock and mounts it on the end of his barn in Nottinghamshire. British Rail later takes casts from it to make a replica.
- **2007** – Reopens as St Pancras International after a major restoration and extension for Eurostar trains, with a new clock by Dent copied from Hoggard's original.
- **2011** – The hotel reopens as the St Pancras Renaissance Hotel.
- **2022** – The Hardy Tree, surrounded by gravestones from the 1860s clearance, falls.

## The alternative view

Each point below carries an evidence label: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative** (see the [research brief](https://github.com/martinv678/our-old-world/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

### An undercroft below the tracks

**Documented** (the design) / **Speculative** (a mud flood reading) – The station stands on a huge undercroft, a hall of several hundred iron columns under the platforms. In old world research, large vaulted spaces under buildings are often linked to mud flood theories. Here, though, the undercroft is at street level by design: it was the tracks that were raised, to cross the Regent's Canal. That makes St Pancras a useful test case. If a deliberately raised building can produce a "lower level" like this, the same explanation should be checked before reading buried levels elsewhere as signs of a flood.

### Built for beer barrels

**Reported** – The spacing of the undercroft's columns is often said to have been set by the size of a Burton beer barrel, with sources giving figures such as 14 ft between columns. Column counts vary from about 690 to 800. A whole station laid out around a beer barrel is a striking claim. Is it in Barlow's records, or a later story?

### What was cleared away

**Documented** – To make room for the station, the railway removed a whole neighbourhood, Agar Town, within about two months of buying it in 1866, and exhumed part of a churchyard in 1865. The future novelist Thomas Hardy oversaw the exhumation. Agar Town's reputation as a slum has since been questioned by historians. In old world research, clearing and rebuilding whole districts in the 1800s is one of the patterns linked to the idea of a reset: what was lost, and how well was it recorded?

### The Hardy Tree

**Documented** – Hundreds of displaced gravestones were stacked around an ash tree in the churchyard, which became known as the Hardy Tree. Whether Hardy himself arranged them is not documented. The tree stood for over 150 years before falling in 2022. It is one of the few visible traces of what the railway removed.

### The clock: smashed, rebuilt in a barn, copied

**Documented** (fall and rebuild) / **Reported** (the American sale) – The great clock was taken down and smashed in the 1970s. The widely told story says it was being sold to an American collector when it fell. The station's own history (HS1) says there is no documentary evidence for that sale. What is documented is that Roland Hoggard bought the pieces for £25 and rebuilt the clock on his barn, and that today's clock is a copy of it. So the clock visitors see is a replica of a rebuilt original, and the story told about its loss may not be true. This is a good example of how quickly a building's history can drift.

### A design used twice

**Reported** – Scott is often said to have recycled ideas from his rejected Gothic design for government offices in Whitehall. As with the claim that Villa Sultana was based on the Monte Carlo Casino, it raises the question of how many grand buildings of the period came from shared or reused designs.

### A grand building with a short life

**Documented** – The hotel opened in 1873 and closed as a hotel in 1935, around 60 years later. It was then nearly demolished in the 1960s. As with other buildings in this repo, a huge, expensive building with a short working life raises the question of whether its stated purpose is the full story.

### Iron and glass on a giant scale

**Documented** (dates) / **Speculative** (an older origin) – Barlow's roof, the largest single span of its time, was built in about three years. Like the Crystal Palace, it's part of a wave of huge iron and glass structures that the alternative view sees as possibly reusing older knowledge. Unusually, here the maker and date are cast into the iron itself.

### The shed was photographed; the hotel apparently wasn't

**Documented** (shed) / **Observed** (no hotel photographs found so far) – Photographs exist of the train shed being built: the basement in 1867 and the roof ribs going up in 1868. But no photograph of Scott's Gothic hotel under construction has been found yet, even though it was built right next to the shed over the following eight years. This fits the wider pattern in the project's [photo record](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#missing-construction-photographs-the-photo-record-argument): iron and engineering works were photographed while going up, while Gothic stone and brick buildings of the same years, such as [Manchester Town Hall](../manchester-town-hall/manchester-town-hall.md) and the [Royal Courts of Justice](../royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md), so far have no construction photographs. For the alternative view, St Pancras is the sharpest test case: the same client, the same site and the same years, with one half photographed and the other not (**Speculative**).

Points to weigh up:

- **The shed was the engineering showpiece.** Its record-breaking span was of interest to engineers and journals, which is a likely reason it was photographed.
- **The hotel was built in stages** from 1868 to 1876 while the station was in use. Progress views may exist as engravings in *The Builder* or *The Building News* rather than as photographs.
- **The Midland Railway's archive hasn't been searched.** The National Railway Museum and the National Archives hold its records, which may include photographs.

**How to test it:** search the National Railway Museum's collections and the Midland Railway records at the National Archives for images dated 1868–76, and *The Builder* and *The Building News* for engravings of the hotel works.

### Materials brought in on the railway's own line

**Documented** (sources) / **Reported** (quantities) – The station was built from Midland materials carried on the Midland Railway's own line: deep red Gripper's patent bricks from Nottingham, Ketton and Ancaster limestone, red Mansfield sandstone and ironwork from the Butterley Company in Derbyshire. Demand for the Nottingham bricks is said to have outrun supply, so more had to be ordered from Tucker & Sons of Loughborough. One source puts the total at about 50 million bricks for the shed and hotel. This is a good example for the [logistics argument](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#materials-and-delivery-the-logistics-argument): the railway could deliver its own building materials by train, straight to the site. See the delivery check under [Timeline check](#timeline-check).

### Timeline check

**What we know:**

- The train shed was built in about three years (1865–1868).
- The hotel took about eight years (1868–1876), opening in 1873 while still unfinished.
- One source gives about 50 million bricks for the whole station and hotel (not confirmed).

**The working:**

- **The roof:** a series of wrought-iron arch ribs, all of the same design, made by the Butterley Company in Derbyshire and brought in by rail. Repeating one standard rib many times is the same method that made the Crystal Palace fast. The undercroft columns are likewise standard castings.
- **Brickwork:** 50 million bricks over about 11 years (1865–1876) is about 4.5 million a year, or about 15,000 a day over 300 working days. At the commonly quoted Victorian rate of 500–1,000 bricks per bricklayer per day (not confirmed here), that's about 15 to 30 bricklayers laying at once.
- **Delivery check:** 15,000 bricks a day at about 3 kg each is about 45 tonnes a day. If it all went by road, that's about 30 one-horse loads a day at 500 bricks, or about 50 at 300 bricks (the load size is uncertain). But here the materials came by rail on the Midland's own line: at a rough 6–10 tonnes per 1860s goods wagon (not confirmed here), that's about 5 to 8 wagon-loads of bricks a day. The yearly need, about 4.5 million bricks, is about two-thirds of one per cent of the roughly 700 million bricks London used each year around 1879. See the [logistics argument](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#materials-and-delivery-the-logistics-argument) for the method.
- **Strain on supply:** the Nottingham brickmaker reportedly couldn't keep up, so a second supplier was brought in. That suggests the demand was real and large enough to stretch a specialist maker.

**Working still needed:**

1. A confirmed brick count, and the tonnage of stone and iron.
2. The size of the workforce for the shed and for the hotel.
3. The Midland Railway's goods records for materials delivered to the site.

**Verdict for now:** **Plausible.** Three years for a repeated-rib iron roof, with the manufacturer's name and year cast into it, fits the industry of the 1860s, and the railway could deliver its own materials by train. Eight years for the hotel is not fast. The stronger questions here are what was removed to build it, why the hotel apparently wasn't photographed while the shed was, and how the station's own history has been retold, as the clock story shows.

### What would support the alternative view

- Signs that the undercroft or parts of the site were in use before 1865
- Structures below the undercroft not explained by the canal crossing
- Gaps in the Midland Railway's building records
- Records of what was found when Agar Town and the churchyard were cleared that don't match the official story

### What would weaken it

- Midland Railway board minutes, contracts and drawings for the shed and undercroft
- The Butterley Company's records for the ironwork
- Dated photographs and engravings of the construction
- Survey records from the 2000s restoration
- Photographs or engravings of the hotel under construction

## Open questions for contributors

- **The undercroft:** Is the beer-barrel spacing documented in Barlow's records? How many columns are there?
- **Roof span:** 240 ft or 245 ft 6 in?
- **Agar Town:** Was it cleared in 1866 or 1868, and how many people lived there?
- **The churchyard:** How many graves were moved, where were the remains taken, and did Hardy arrange the stones around the tree?
- **The clock:** When exactly was it taken down, and how did it fall?
- **Scott's design:** How much of the hotel really comes from his Whitehall scheme?
- **Hotel construction photographs:** Do any photographs of the hotel being built exist, in the National Railway Museum or elsewhere?
- **Brick count:** How many bricks were used, and how many came from Nottingham and Loughborough?
- **Photos:** Photos of Agar Town, the churchyard clearance and the construction would be especially useful.
