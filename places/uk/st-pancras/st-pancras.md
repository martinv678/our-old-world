## Overview

St Pancras is a railway station in north London with two famous parts. At the back is William Barlow's huge arched iron and glass train shed. At the front, facing Euston Road, is George Gilbert Scott's red-brick Gothic hotel, with its spires and clock tower. Today it is the London terminal for Eurostar trains to Europe.

## The main story

- **1863–1865** – The Midland Railway plans its own London terminus. Barlow designs the train shed.
- **1868** – The station opens. Barlow's arched roof is the largest single-span roof in the world at the time. The tracks are raised about 5 m above street level, and the space underneath, the undercroft, is used to store beer barrels from Burton upon Trent. The spacing of its iron columns is often said to have been based on the size of a beer barrel.
- **1868–1876** – Scott's Midland Grand Hotel is built in stages in front of the shed. Sources give completion as 1873 or 1876.
- **1935** – The hotel closes, having fallen out of date, and becomes railway offices, renamed St Pancras Chambers.
- **1960s** – The station and hotel are threatened with demolition. A campaign, led in part by the poet John Betjeman, saves them, and they are listed Grade I in 1967.
- **2007** – Reopens as St Pancras International after a major restoration and extension for Eurostar trains.
- **2011** – The hotel reopens as the St Pancras Renaissance Hotel.

## The alternative view

### An undercroft below the tracks

The official story is that the station was raised on a huge undercroft so that tracks could cross the Regent's Canal, and the space below was used for beer. In old world research, large vaulted spaces under buildings, below street level, are often linked to mud flood theories. Was the undercroft really built as a basement, or was it once at ground level?

### The clock tower

Clock towers appear in very similar forms across the world in the same period. St Pancras's clock tower is one of the most prominent in London. What is the clock's history, and what was the tower used for?

### A grand building with a short life

The hotel opened in the 1870s and closed as a hotel in 1935, around 60 years later. It was then nearly demolished in the 1960s. As with other buildings in this repo, a huge, expensive building with a short working life raises the question of whether its stated purpose is the full story.

### Iron and glass on a giant scale

Barlow's roof, the largest single span of its time, was built in only a few years. Like the Crystal Palace, it's part of a wave of huge iron and glass structures that the alternative view sees as possibly reusing older knowledge or older structures.

### What would support the alternative view

- Signs that the undercroft was once open to daylight or at street level
- Structures below the undercroft not explained by the canal crossing
- Gaps in the Midland Railway's building records

### What would weaken it

- Midland Railway board minutes, contracts and drawings for the shed and undercroft
- Dated photographs and engravings of the construction
- Survey records from the 2000s restoration
