## Overview

Alexandra Palace, known as "Ally Pally", stands on a hilltop in Alexandra Park in north London. It was planned in the late 1850s as north London's answer to the Crystal Palace at Sydenham, and it was built partly from the materials of the demolished 1862 International Exhibition building in South Kensington. The first palace opened on 24 May 1873 and burned down 16 days later, on 9 June 1873. A larger, redesigned palace by the same architect and builders opened on 1 May 1875, less than two years later.

Since then the building has been an entertainment centre, a refugee shelter and internment camp in the First World War, and the home of the BBC's first regular high-definition television service in 1936. A second great fire on 10 July 1980 destroyed about a third of the building, including the Great Hall and its famous Willis organ. The rebuilt palace reopened in 1988. Its Victorian theatre, with rare surviving stage machinery, reopened in 2018.

## The main story

The palace was conceived as a "People's Palace" for north London: a place of cheap, improving entertainment reached by railway, to rival the Crystal Palace in the south.

- **December 1858 – 1859** – The designer Owen Jones exhibits plans for a vast glass "Palace of the People" on Muswell Hill. It's never built in that form. (Sources give both 1858 and 1859 for his proposal.)
- **c. 1860** – The Great Northern Palace Company is formed but at first fails to raise the money to build.
- **23 July 1863** – Alexandra Park opens to the public. The palace and park are named after Alexandra of Denmark, who married the Prince of Wales that year.
- **1864** – The 1862 International Exhibition building in South Kensington, which the government declined to keep, is demolished. Its materials are acquired for the Muswell Hill project. The contractors Kelk and Lucas (John Kelk with Charles and Thomas Lucas), who had built the 1862 building, are involved, and Kelk puts a large amount of his own money into the scheme.
- **1865–1866** – Building begins (sources give 1864, September 1865 and September 1866) to designs by Alfred Meeson and John Johnson. Johnson keeps some of Jones's ideas, such as the nave and transept arrangement. Work then stalls for several years because of money problems, leaving the building half finished.
- **1868** – A racecourse opens in the park. Nicknamed the "Frying Pan" for its tight loop, it's London's only racecourse until it closes in 1970.
- **24 May 1873** – The palace opens, on Queen Victoria's birthday, together with the Muswell Hill Railway's Alexandra Palace station. The building has a central dome, reported as 220 ft high.
- **9 June 1873** – Sixteen days after opening, the palace burns down. Contemporary accounts blame a cinder or red-hot charcoal from a plumber's brazier used by workmen repairing the roof. Three members of staff die. Within about two hours only the outer walls are left standing.
- **1873–1875** – The palace is rebuilt by the same architect and builders to a larger, different design. The central dome is not replaced; instead the Great Hall gets a curved roof about 85 ft wide, and four corner towers hold water tanks (reported as 16,000 gallons each) in case of another fire. The final cost is reported as about £417,000.
- **1 May 1875** – The second palace opens, covering about 7 to 7.5 acres. It contains a Great Hall seating about 10,000 (figures vary) with a new Willis concert organ, which "Father" Henry Willis is said to have thought his finest, and a theatre with a deep cellar of stage machinery for scene changes, traps and flying effects.
- **1900** – A group of local councils led by Hornsey Urban District Council buys the palace and park. An Act of Parliament creates the Alexandra Palace and Park Trust and requires the site to remain "available for the free use and recreation of the public forever". (One Haringey document gives 1901.)
- **1914–1919** – The palace shelters Belgian refugees, then from 1915 to 1919 serves as an internment camp for German and Austrian civilian men, with up to about 3,000 held at any one time. The internees include many artists and musicians, who form a camp orchestra.
- **1922** – After wartime damage and alterations, the trustees reopen the palace for music and entertainment.
- **1935–1936** – The trustees lease the south-east part of the building to the BBC. A transmitting mast is built on the south-east tower, and on **2 November 1936** the BBC launches the world's first regular high-definition public television service from the palace.
- **1954** – Passenger services to Alexandra Palace station end (sources give 3 or 5 July 1954).
- **1960s** – The Greater London Council becomes trustee.
- **10 July 1980** – Trusteeship passes to the London Borough of Haringey. On the same day a fire, which the palace's own account says probably started behind the Willis organ, spreads through the roof and destroys about a third of the building: the Great Hall, the banqueting suite and the former roller rink. The Palm Court and the BBC wing survive.
- **17 March 1988** – The rebuilt palace formally reopens, with a new Great Hall and West Hall and a restored Palm Court. The work is funded partly by insurance.
- **1 December 2018** – After a six-year project, the theatre reopens in a state of "arrested decay", with its Victorian stage machinery conserved but not in use.

## Conservation

- The palace (including the site of the former station to the north) is **Grade II listed** by Historic England (list entry 1268256), and Alexandra Park is on the **Register of Parks and Gardens** (list entry 1001253).
- The **Alexandra Palace and Park Act 1900** still binds the site: the building and park must remain available for the free use and recreation of the public. The London Borough of Haringey is the trust's sole corporate trustee.
- After the 1980 fire the Great Hall, West Hall and Palm Court were rebuilt and restored in 1980–88, funded partly by insurance.
- A later National Lottery grant of nearly £19 million helped later restoration work. The theatre reopened on 1 December 2018 in "arrested decay" rather than as a full restoration, keeping its Victorian stage machinery in place.

## The alternative view

The official account is of a Victorian commercial venture that was unlucky: slow to build for lack of money, burned by accident just after opening, rebuilt quickly, and burned again a century later. Looked at from an "old world" perspective, several things invite a second look: a building made from another building, a dome that vanished, a fast rebuild with no clear money behind it, and two great fires at telling moments. Each point below carries an evidence label: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative** (see the [research brief](https://github.com/martinv678/our-old-world/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

### Burned sixteen days after opening

**Documented** (dates) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – Alexandra Palace is one of the most striking examples of the "grand building lost to fire soon after completion" pattern. It took around eight years to get from the start of building to the opening, and then the palace was destroyed in about two hours, 16 days later. The official cause, a cinder from a workman's brazier on the roof, is plausible for a building full of timber, but it rests on newspaper accounts of the day rather than any formal inquiry that has been traced so far. In alternative history research, a fire that removes a newly "finished" building is exactly the kind of event to examine closely: what was actually lost, and was the building that people saw on 24 May 1873 really new?

Points to weigh up:

- **The cause is ordinary.** Plumbers working on lead roofs used braziers to heat their irons and solder, and fires started this way were common in the period.
- **People died.** Three staff were killed, which would normally mean an inquest. Its records, if they survive, would give sworn evidence about where and how the fire started.
- **The fire was widely reported.** Newspapers covered it in detail, so the extent of the damage can be checked against what was rebuilt.

**How to test it:** find the inquest records for the three deaths, any fire brigade report, and images of the ruins, and compare what they say survived with what was rebuilt.

### A building made from another building

**Documented** (reuse) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – The palace was built partly from the materials of the 1862 International Exhibition building in South Kensington, which was taken down in 1864. That building, designed by Captain Francis Fowke, had itself been criticised and was demolished after only one exhibition season. So the story of Alexandra Palace begins with a great hall being dismantled, moved across London and rebuilt in a different form, just as the Crystal Palace was moved from Hyde Park to Sydenham. The alternative view asks how much of each "new" exhibition palace was really new, and how much was recycled from structures whose own origins deserve checking. Detailed comparison of the 1862 ironwork with the surviving fabric at Alexandra Palace would test this.

### Rebuilt bigger, in under two years

**Documented** – The first palace took about eight years from the start of work to the opening, with long stoppages for lack of money. Yet after the 1873 fire a larger, redesigned palace was completed in under 23 months, by the same team, while the company was already short of funds and the first building had been under-insured. See the [Timeline check](#timeline-check). The contrast between the slow first build and the very fast rebuild is one of the main questions for this entry.

### The lost dome

**Reported** – The 1873 palace had a central dome, reported as 220 ft high. The 1875 palace has no central dome; the Great Hall has a curved roof instead, and the building is marked by four corner towers. Domes are central to the "lost energy technology" theme in this project. Why was the dome not rebuilt, when almost everything else was rebuilt larger? The official answer is presumably cost and fire safety, but no source reached so far explains the decision. Old engravings and photographs of the 1873 dome are a priority to find.

**How to test it:** find dated images of the 1873 palace from several angles and check:

- the dome's size and shape against the reported 220 ft height
- whether the dome appears in the photographs of the ruins after the fire, and in what state
- whether the company's minutes or *The Builder* give a reason for leaving it out of the rebuild

### Water towers on the corners

**Reported** – The four corner towers of the 1875 palace are said to have held large water tanks, reported as 16,000 gallons each, so that a fire could be put out quickly. This is a sensible response to the 1873 disaster. It's also worth noting that the Crystal Palace had its own giant water towers, and both buildings later lost large parts of themselves to fire anyway.

### Under-insured and uninsured

**Reported** – A report in *The Spectator* of 14 June 1873 says the first palace had cost about half a million pounds and was insured for only about £200,000 (the figure is read from an imperfect digitised scan), and that the stallholders and employees appear to have been uninsured. A new building, open for barely two weeks and insured for well under half its cost, is a detail worth checking against company and insurance records.

### A second fire on the day of a handover

**Reported** – The trusteeship of the palace passed from the Greater London Council to the London Borough of Haringey on 10 July 1980, the same day the second great fire destroyed the Great Hall. This coincidence is stated in a source found by search but not yet checked against the primary records. If confirmed, it would be a notable "convenient fire": the building was badly damaged on the very day responsibility for it changed hands. The cause of the 1980 fire has not been confirmed in the sources reached so far (**Speculative** as to any wider meaning).

### A tower for television

**Documented** (mast and broadcasts) / **Speculative** (energy theme) – In 1936 a tall transmitting mast was raised on the south-east tower, and the palace became the birthplace of regular high-definition television. For this project's "lost energy technology" theme, it's interesting that a hilltop palace with towers was chosen as a transmitter. The ordinary explanation is height: the hill is one of the highest points in north London, which suits broadcasting. The same pattern appears at the Crystal Palace, where John Logie Baird used the south water tower for experiments from 1933. Both are hilltop exhibition palaces with towers that became broadcasting sites.

### A theatre frozen in time

**Documented** – The Victorian theatre was closed to the public for about 80 years and its stage machinery survived unusually intact in a deep cellar beneath the stage. It reopened in 2018 in a state of "arrested decay" rather than full restoration. This makes it one of the best places in the building to examine original Victorian fabric directly, including construction methods, materials and any date marks or inscriptions.

### Ground levels and the hillside

**Speculative** – The palace sits on a slope, with terraces in front. In mud flood research, lower levels, cellars and terraces on hillside buildings are checked for signs of buried windows or doors. No evidence of this has been found for Alexandra Palace, and the slope is a natural explanation for any lower levels. Comparing the earliest engravings and 1870s photographs of the south front with the building today would settle it. The theatre cellar (about 22 ft deep) should be checked against the original plans.

### Part of a pattern of "people's palaces"

**Documented** (fires) / **Speculative** (pattern) – Alexandra Palace belongs to a family of Victorian exhibition and entertainment palaces that suffered repeated fires:

- the [Crystal Palace](../crystal-palace/crystal-palace.md) lost its north transept in 1866 and burned completely in 1936
- the New York Crystal Palace burned in 1858
- the Munich Glaspalast burned in 1931
- Alexandra Palace burned in 1873 and again in 1980

Large buildings full of timber, exhibits and gaslight were genuinely at risk. But repeated fires that remove or reshape grand buildings are one of the main patterns this project sets out to test.

### Dates and inscriptions

**Speculative** – No date stones, foundation stones or inscriptions on the building have been checked yet for the "letters in dates" argument (see [ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md)). The 1875 building, the theatre and any surviving fabric from the 1862 exhibition building should be searched for dated plaques and for how the years are written.

### Timeline check

**What we know:** the first palace was rebuilt between the fire on 9 June 1873 and the reopening on 1 May 1875: about 98 weeks, or just under 23 months. The new palace covered about 7 to 7.5 acres and cost about £417,000.

**The working:**

- **Floor area:** 7 acres is about 28,300 m², or about 305,000 sq ft. Over about 95 weeks of building (allowing a few weeks to clear the ruins), that's roughly 300 m² of finished building a week.
- **Re-using the shell:** reports say the outer walls of the first palace survived the fire. If they were reused, much of the slowest work (foundations and brick walls) was already done. This needs confirming: were the 1873 walls kept, partly kept or demolished?
- **Ironwork and roofs:** the Great Hall roof, about 85 ft wide, and other roofs would have been factory-made iron trusses and glazing, bolted together on site, much as at the Crystal Palace.
- **Builders:** Kelk and Lucas were among the largest contractors in Britain and were building the Royal Albert Hall (1867–1871) at about the same period. They had both the workforce and the experience. Workforce figures for the rebuild haven't been found yet.
- **Money:** £417,000 in about 23 months is about £18,000 a month. Where this money came from, given that the first palace was under-insured and the company had struggled for years to raise funds, is an open question.

**Verdict for now:** **Tight but plausible**, on the assumption that the surviving walls and foundations were reused. The speed of the rebuild is less striking than the contrast with the first build, which stalled for years. The bigger questions are about the money, the decision to drop the dome, and the circumstances of both fires.

**Working still needed:**

1. Whether the 1873 outer walls and foundations were reused, from the rebuild contracts or *The Builder*.
2. The size of the workforce on the rebuild, from newspaper reports or company records.
3. Where the £417,000 came from: insurance, new shares, loans or the contractors themselves.
4. Floor area and roof spans from Johnson and Meeson's drawings, to replace the rough acreage used above.

### What would support the alternative view

- Evidence that parts of the 1873 or 1875 palace existed before the official construction dates, or came from an earlier structure whose origin can't be traced
- Contradictions between the reported fire damage in 1873 and what was actually rebuilt (for example, walls described as destroyed that show older fabric)
- No inquest or inquiry into the three deaths in 1873, or records that contradict the brazier explanation
- Confirmation that the 1980 fire happened on the day of the trusteeship handover, together with an unexplained or disputed cause
- Buried windows, doors or floors on the hillside levels, shown in early images

### What would weaken it

- Inquest records for the three staff who died in 1873, and fire brigade reports for 1873 and 1980
- The building company's accounts, contracts and insurance records showing how the rebuild was paid for
- Johnson and Meeson's drawings for both palaces, and records of the purchase and transport of the 1862 Exhibition materials
- Dated photographs and engravings of each stage of construction in 1865–73 and 1873–75
- The many detailed reports in the *Illustrated London News*, *The Builder* and *The Times* of 1873–75

## Open questions for contributors

- **Start date:** Did building begin in 1864, September 1865 or September 1866?
- **The 1873 dome:** How high was it really, what was it made of, and why was it not rebuilt?
- **The 1873 fire:** Was there an inquest into the three deaths, and what did it find? What exactly survived?
- **The walls:** Were the outer walls of the first palace reused in the 1875 building?
- **The 1862 materials:** Which parts of the 1862 Exhibition building ended up at Muswell Hill, and can any still be identified?
- **Money:** How was the £417,000 rebuild funded so quickly?
- **The 1980 fire:** What was the official cause, and did it really happen on the day trusteeship changed hands?
- **Great Hall capacity:** Sources give figures from about 10,000 to 14,000.
- **Station closure:** Did the last trains run on 3 or 5 July 1954?
