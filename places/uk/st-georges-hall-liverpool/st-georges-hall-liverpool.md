## Overview

St George's Hall stands on a raised plateau opposite Lime Street station in the centre of Liverpool. It's a vast classical temple of a building, with long rows of Corinthian columns, containing a great concert hall, a smaller concert room and, until 1984, the city's law courts. It has been called one of the finest neoclassical buildings in the world.

Its story is unusual from the start. The foundation stone was laid in 1838, on Queen Victoria's coronation day, before the building had even been designed. The competition was then won by a 25-year-old architect, Harvey Lonsdale Elmes, who died in 1847, seven years before it opened. Inside, the Great Hall is roofed by a huge barrel vault made of hollow bricks, its floor is made of about 30,000 Minton tiles, and the building was fitted with one of the earliest systems of mechanical heating and ventilation in a British public building.

## The main story

Liverpool was growing rich as a port, and wanted a hall for its music festivals and public meetings, and new courts for the assizes.

- **28 June 1838** – A foundation stone is laid for a new hall on Queen Victoria's coronation day, as part of the city's celebrations. No design has yet been chosen. The stone is not part of the building as finally built.
- **July 1839** – Harvey Lonsdale Elmes, aged 25, wins the competition for the hall against 86 other entries.
- **1840** – Elmes also wins a separate competition for new assize courts. The two projects are combined into one building.
- **1841** – Building begins (some sources say 1842). Dr David Boswell Reid is appointed as consulting engineer for heating and ventilation in July 1841.
- **1840s** – The building goes up on a raised platform, with basement rooms for the heating and ventilation plant. The Great Hall's barrel vault is designed by the engineer Robert Rawlinson using hollow clay pots, to reduce its weight and to let air pass from the hall into the roof space as part of the ventilation system.
- **26 November 1847** – Elmes dies, aged 33, before the building is finished. Rawlinson takes over the engineering.
- **1851** – Charles Robert Cockerell takes over the design and supervision, and completes the interiors and the sculpture scheme.
- **18 September 1854** – The hall opens. Its floor of about 30,000 Minton encaustic tiles shows the city's coat of arms, sea nymphs, dolphins and tritons. The cost is given as about £290,000.
- **1855** – A great organ by Henry Willis is installed in the Great Hall, said to have been the largest in Britain at the time (**Reported**).
- **1856** – The small concert room is completed.
- **August 1950** – Large pieces of stone fall from the sculpture in the south pediment. The sculpture is removed and put into storage, and the pediment has been empty ever since.
- **1984** – The law courts move out, and the building stands largely unused for some years.
- **About 2007** – A restoration costing about £23 million is completed, and the hall reopens as a public venue.

## Conservation

- St George's Hall is **Grade I listed** (Historic England list entry 1361677).
- The Minton floor is normally covered to protect it, and is uncovered for public viewing on special occasions.
- The south pediment remains empty, and there have been campaigns to replace its sculpture.

## The alternative view

The official account is of a confident Victorian port city building a Greek temple for music and justice. Looked at from an "old world" perspective, several things invite a second look: a foundation stone laid before there was a design, a young architect who died before it was finished, a hidden mechanical system for moving air through the building, a building set on a raised plateau above its surroundings, and sculpture that was removed and never replaced. Each point below carries an evidence label: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative** (see the [research brief](https://github.com/martinv678/our-old-world/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

### A foundation stone for a building that didn't exist yet

**Documented** – The foundation stone was laid on 28 June 1838, a year before the design competition was decided, and two buildings were later combined into one with a new design. The stone did not form part of the final building. A ceremony for a building that hadn't been designed, and a stone that then disappears from the story, is exactly the kind of record this project watches for under "missing or convenient records". The ordinary explanation is civic celebration: the coronation was a good occasion for a ceremony, and the scheme changed afterwards.

**How to test it:** where is the 1838 stone now? Is it recorded in the council minutes, buried somewhere on the site, or lost? What inscription and date does it carry, and how is the date written (see the [letters in dates](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#letters-in-dates-the-j-argument) argument)?

### The architect who died before it was finished

**Documented** – Elmes won the competition at 25 and died in 1847 at 33, seven years before the opening. The building was finished by an engineer, Rawlinson, and another architect, Cockerell, who designed much of the interior. This is the earliest of a series of buildings in this project whose designers died before completion:

- Harvey Lonsdale Elmes: St George's Hall (died 1847)
- Captain Francis Fowke: [Royal Albert Hall](../royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md) and [Natural History Museum](../natural-history-museum/natural-history-museum.md) (died 1865)
- Giuseppe Mengoni: [Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II](../../italy/galleria-vittorio-emanuele-ii/galleria-vittorio-emanuele-ii.md) (died 1877)
- George Edmund Street: [Royal Courts of Justice](../royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md) (died 1881)
- Horace Jones: [Tower Bridge](../tower-bridge/tower-bridge.md) (died 1887)

Elmes is said to have died of tuberculosis, worn out by the work (**Reported**). As with the others, long projects make this pattern likely to be coincidence (**Speculative**), but it means that again the finished building was partly the work of others.

### A building that breathes

**Documented** (system) / **Speculative** (energy theme) – Dr David Boswell Reid designed a system of heating and ventilation for the hall: fans in the basement drove air through the building, warming or cooling it, and the hollow-pot bricks of the Great Hall's vault let air pass into the roof space. It's often described as one of the first attempts at air conditioning in a British public building (that claim is widely repeated but not independently checked). Reid had earlier worked on the ventilation of the Houses of Parliament.

For this project's "lost energy technology" theme, this is another genuine, documented system. Like the ventilation towers at the [Natural History Museum](../natural-history-museum/natural-history-museum.md#towers-that-breathe), the hydraulics at [Tower Bridge](../tower-bridge/tower-bridge.md#towers-full-of-stored-power) and the coal tunnel at [Kew](../palm-house-kew/palm-house-kew.md#a-chimney-disguised-as-a-bell-tower-and-a-tunnel-with-a-railway), it shows what a real 19th-century system leaves behind: basement plant rooms, ducts, flues and records. It also shows that Victorian engineers were thinking carefully about air, heat and comfort in large buildings.

### Raised on a plateau, with rooms below

**Observed** (plateau and basement) / **Speculative** (mud flood) – The hall stands on a raised platform, reached by steps, above the surrounding streets and squares, and has extensive rooms below the main floor: the plant for the heating and ventilation, and spaces linked to the former courts. In mud flood research, buildings with lower floors and raised platforms are checked for signs that the ground around them was once lower. Here, the plateau and the basement were part of the design, which needed space below for Reid's system, and the land falls away around the site. No evidence of buried former ground floors has been found.

**How to test it:** compare the earliest engravings and photographs of the hall and the surrounding streets with the site today, and the original plans of the basement with what survives.

### The sculpture that was removed

**Documented** – The south pediment once held a sculpture group designed by Cockerell and carved by William Nicholl. In August 1950, after large pieces of stone fell from it (some reported as weighing about 50 lb), it was taken down and put into storage. One account blames general decay; another says an expert judged it "decayed and defaced". The pediment has stood empty for more than 70 years. This fits the project's theme of lost and changed parts: what was the sculpture made of, why did it decay so fast, and where are the pieces now?

### A Greek temple in a Victorian port

**Documented** (style) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – The hall is modelled on Greek and Roman temples, with giant Corinthian columns and a coffered barrel vault like those of Roman baths. In the "shared worldwide architecture" theme, the same classical forms appear on government buildings, banks, museums and churches across Europe, Russia and the Americas in the same decades, for example [Saint Isaac's Cathedral](../../russia/saint-isaacs-cathedral/saint-isaacs-cathedral.md) in St Petersburg (1818–58). The ordinary explanation is the Greek Revival, a well-documented movement in architecture.

### Sound: a concert hall with a great organ

**Documented** (organ) / **Speculative** (cymatics) – The Great Hall was built for music and has a Willis organ installed in 1855. For the [sound and cymatics](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#sound-and-cymatics-the-resonance-argument) theme, it is worth recording the hall's acoustics, the organ's size and tuning, and whether any measurements of the hall's resonance have been made.

### No construction images found

**Observed** (none found so far) – No photographs or engravings of St George's Hall being built have been found yet, although building went on from 1841 to 1854 in the centre of a large city, in the years when photography was spreading. See the [photo record](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#missing-construction-photographs-the-photo-record-argument).

### Timeline check

**What we know:** building began in 1841 (or 1842) and the hall opened on 18 September 1854, about 13 years, with the small concert room finished in 1856. The Great Hall is about 169 ft long, 77 ft wide and 82 ft high to the crown of the vault. The cost is given as about £290,000.

**The working:**

- **Time:** 13 years is long. It includes a change of plan (combining two buildings), the death of the architect and a change of designer, which all slow a project down.
- **Method:** the building is load-bearing stone and brick, with a vault of hollow clay pots, which is slow, hand-built work. That fits the long timeline.
- **Delivery check:** the quantities of stone and brick haven't been found yet. Liverpool was one of the world's great ports, with docks, canals and railways, so stone and brick could have come in by sea, canal or rail.

**Working still needed:**

1. The quantities of stone, brick and clay pots, from the contracts.
2. The size of the workforce.
3. Which parts were built when, from the council's records.

**Verdict for now:** **Plausible, and slow.** Like the [Royal Courts of Justice](../royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md), this is a long stone build with recorded delays and changes. The questions for the alternative view are the 1838 foundation stone, the architect's death, the missing pediment sculpture and the missing construction images, not the speed.

### What would support the alternative view

- Evidence that the platform, basement or lower walls are older than 1841
- The 1838 foundation stone turning out to be missing, or recorded with an inconsistent date
- Gaps or contradictions in the council's records of the design and construction
- No engravings or reports of the works in progress in the 1840s

### What would weaken it

- The council minutes and building committee records, 1838–56
- Elmes's, Rawlinson's and Cockerell's drawings (the Royal Academy and RIBA hold designs, including for the pediment)
- Reid's published descriptions of the heating and ventilation
- Engravings and reports in the *Illustrated London News*, *The Builder* and Liverpool newspapers
