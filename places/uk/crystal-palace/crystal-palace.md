## Overview

The Crystal Palace was a vast glass and iron hall built in Hyde Park for the Great Exhibition of 1851. After the exhibition it was taken down and rebuilt, even bigger, on Sydenham Hill in south London, where it stood as an entertainment and exhibition centre until it burned down in 1936. The area is still called Crystal Palace, and the park keeps the terraces, stone sphinxes and the famous Victorian dinosaur sculptures.

## The main story

- **1850** – After hundreds of rejected designs, the exhibition committee accepts a plan by Joseph Paxton, a gardener and glasshouse designer, based on his greenhouses at Chatsworth. Building starts in Hyde Park in the late summer or autumn of 1850.
- **1851** – The building is finished in roughly nine months using standardised, factory-made iron and glass parts. Sources put the glass at around 293,000–300,000 panes, made by Chance Brothers of Birmingham. The Great Exhibition opens on 1 May 1851 and draws around six million visitors.
- **1852–1854** – The building is taken down and rebuilt, in an enlarged form, on Sydenham Hill. It reopens in June 1854 with landscaped grounds, fountains and two water towers by Isambard Kingdom Brunel.
- **1854–1936** – Used for concerts, exhibitions, sport and entertainment. It struggles financially for much of this time.
- **30 November 1936** – A fire destroys the main building in a few hours, watched by huge crowds. The cause has never been established.
- **1941** – Brunel's two water towers, which survived the fire, are demolished during the Second World War, as they were thought to be a landmark for German bombers.

## The alternative view

### Built in nine months

The official story is that a building covering about 19 acres, with hundreds of thousands of panes of glass, was designed, manufactured and put up in about nine months in 1850–51. For many in the alternative history community this is the classic example of a timeline that seems too fast. Was it really built from scratch, or was an existing structure, or existing technology, simply moved and presented as new?

### Moved, rebuilt and enlarged

The building was then taken down and rebuilt, larger, on a different site in under three years. The same question applies: how much of the Sydenham palace was really new?

### Lost to fire, then erased

The palace burned down in a single night in 1936, from a cause never identified. The two surviving towers were demolished a few years later. Fires that destroy great exhibition buildings come up often in alternative history research. The Chicago World's Fair buildings of 1893, for example, also burned. Was it bad luck, or part of a pattern of removing older structures?

### Part of a worldwide exhibition era

The Crystal Palace started a wave of huge iron and glass exhibition buildings around the world: New York (1853), Munich (1854), Paris, Melbourne and Madrid, among others. Many of them were temporary, demolished or burned. The alternative view asks whether these "world's fairs" showed off new buildings, or reused older ones.

### What would support the alternative view

- Evidence of the hall's components existing before 1850
- Gaps or contradictions in the manufacturing and delivery records
- Unexplained features in the ruins or foundations at Sydenham

### What would weaken it

- The detailed contracts, drawings and accounts of Fox, Henderson & Co. and Chance Brothers
- The many dated engravings, photographs and newspaper reports of the construction in Hyde Park
- Records of the dismantling and rebuilding at Sydenham

## Open questions for contributors

- **Build dates:** When exactly did work start in Hyde Park: August, September or later in 1850?
- **Glass count:** Sources give figures between about 293,000 and 300,000 panes. Which is right?
- **The fire:** What do the official fire reports say about where and how it started?
- **What survives:** What remains of the foundations, subways and terraces at Sydenham today?
- **Photos:** Historic photos and engravings are likely to be public domain because of their age.
