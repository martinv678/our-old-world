## Overview

The Royal Pavilion is a palace in the centre of Brighton with onion domes, minarets and pinnacles that look as if they belong in India rather than on the English south coast. Inside, it is decorated in a lavish Chinese style. Behind the domes is a cast-iron frame, and beneath it a tunnel runs to the former royal stables. It is now a museum and one of Brighton's best-known landmarks.

## The main story

- **1787** – Henry Holland designs a neoclassical villa, the Marine Pavilion, for George, Prince of Wales, who visits Brighton for its sea air.
- **1803–1808** – Huge royal stables and a riding school are built next to the villa in an Indian style, designed by William Porden, years before the palace itself takes on that look.
- **1815–1823** – George, now Prince Regent, has John Nash transform and greatly enlarge the building into the Indian-inspired palace seen today. Rather than demolish the old house, Nash builds a cast-iron framework around and over it, which carries the domes. The Great Kitchen's ceiling rests on cast-iron columns topped with palm leaves. Sources give the finish date as 1822 or 1823.
- **1820** – George becomes King George IV.
- **1821–1822** – A tunnel is dug from the Pavilion to the stables, cut as a trench across three underground streams, at a cost of about £1,783. It is said to have let the king reach the stables unseen.
- **1830** – George IV dies. William IV uses the Pavilion.
- **1840s** – Queen Victoria visits but dislikes it, finding it too public, and chooses Osborne House on the Isle of Wight as the royal seaside home instead. The Pavilion is stripped of almost all its furniture and interior decoration in expectation of demolition.
- **1850** – Under the Brighton Royal Pavilion Act, the government sells the empty building and grounds to the town of Brighton, usually said to be for £53,000.
- **1860s and later** – Some of the original fittings are returned by Queen Victoria and later monarchs. The stables become a concert hall, the Brighton Dome.
- **1914–1916** – The Pavilion is used as a military hospital for soldiers of the Indian Army wounded on the Western Front.
- **1975** – The Music Room is badly damaged in an arson attack.
- **1987** – In the Great Storm, a stone ball falls from one of the minarets through the roof into the newly restored Music Room.
- **Today** – A museum run by Brighton & Hove Museums. The tunnel and basements can sometimes be visited on guided tours.

## The alternative view

Each point below carries an evidence label: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative** (see the [research brief](https://github.com/martinv678/our-old-world/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

### An Indian palace on the English coast

**Observed** (the style) / **Speculative** (an older origin) – The official story is that one prince's taste for the exotic produced an Indian-style palace in an English seaside town. In alternative history circles, the Pavilion is one of the best-known examples of "out of place" architecture: onion domes and minarets that match buildings across Russia, India and the Middle East. Was it a fashion, or a sign of a shared older building style later explained away as Regency fantasy?

### The stables came first

**Reported** – The Indian-style stables (now the Brighton Dome) were built in 1803–1808, around ten years before Nash gave the palace itself its Indian look. So the most "Indian" building on the site came first, and the palace was later made to match it. Why build the stables in this style before the home they served?

### A cast-iron frame around an older house

**Documented** – Nash did not demolish Holland's 1787 villa. He wrapped it in a cast-iron framework that carries the domes, with the old house inside. That means two buildings of different ages are stacked within one shell, and the iron structure is hidden behind stucco and decoration. How much of what stands today is truly 1815–1823 work, how much is older, and how easy is it to tell from the outside?

### Iron palm trees

**Documented** – The Great Kitchen's ceiling rests on slender cast-iron columns topped with palm-leaf capitals. Iron dressed up as something natural or decorative is a recurring feature of old world buildings. In this case the iron is on show; elsewhere in the building it is hidden.

### The tunnel and the underground streams

**Documented** – The tunnel to the stables was dug in 1821–22 as a trench across three underground streams. Tunnels and underground passages come up often in old world research, and so do hidden water courses. Where exactly do those streams run, what else lies below the Pavilion and its gardens, and are there other passages not shown on public plans?

### Stripped, abandoned, sold

**Documented** – Within about 25 years of its completion, the royal family abandoned the palace, stripped out almost all its contents in expectation of demolition, and sold the empty shell to the town in 1850. A grand building emptied and nearly demolished so soon after it was finished is a pattern seen elsewhere in this project. Why was it built at such cost, then given up so quickly?

### Damage and repair

**Documented** – The Music Room was badly damaged by arson in 1975 and again in the Great Storm of 1987. Each restoration replaces original material. Over time, how much of what visitors see is original Regency work, and how much is modern reconstruction?

### Timeline check

**What we know:** Nash's transformation ran from about 1815 to 1822 or 1823, roughly seven to eight years, under royal funding.

**Points to weigh:**

- It was a remodelling, not a new build: Nash kept Holland's villa and built around it.
- The cast-iron frame was made off site and erected, a method that speeds up construction.
- Much of the time went on the lavish interiors.

**Working still needed:**

1. The total cost and annual spending from the Royal Archives' accounts.
2. The size of the workforce.
3. Which parts of the structure are 1787, which are 1815–23, and which are later repairs.

**Verdict for now:** **Plausible.** Seven or eight years with royal money for a remodelling is not fast. The stronger questions are about the layers inside the building, the stables coming first, the tunnel and what lies beneath, and why it was abandoned so soon.

### What would support the alternative view

- Structure under the domes that predates Holland's 1787 villa
- Underground passages or chambers beyond the known tunnel
- Gaps in the building accounts for Nash's work
- Evidence that the stables, or parts of them, are older than 1803

### What would weaken it

- Nash's published designs and the Royal Archives' building accounts
- Dated drawings and engravings of the building before, during and after the works, including Nash's own *Views of the Royal Pavilion* (1826)
- Records of the tunnel's construction
- Surveys from the 20th-century restorations

## Open questions for contributors

- **Completion date:** 1822 or 1823?
- **The stables:** Were they built in 1803–1808, and did they really take on the Indian style before the palace?
- **The tunnel:** How long is it, where exactly do the underground streams run, and are there other passages?
- **Holland's villa:** How much of the 1787 Marine Pavilion survives inside Nash's building?
- **The iron frame:** Are there drawings of Nash's cast-iron framework, and how much of it can be seen today?
- **Sale price:** £53,000 or £50,000?
- **Photos:** Photos of the tunnel, basements and iron frame would be especially useful.

## Leads to follow up

- **The Royal Archives** and the **Royal Collection Trust** for building accounts, contracts and the inventory of what was removed before 1850.
- **Brighton & Hove Museums**, which run tours of the basements and tunnel and hold the Pavilion's archive.
- **Nash's *Views of the Royal Pavilion* (1826)**, which shows the building as completed; several plates are public domain on Wikimedia Commons.
- **The Keep** (East Sussex archives) for local records, the 1850 sale and the stables.
- **Historic England** listings for the Pavilion, the Dome and the gardens.
- **Restoration reports** from after the 1975 fire and the 1987 storm, which may describe the structure behind the decoration.
