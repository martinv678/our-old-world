## Overview

The Royal Pavilion is a palace in the centre of Brighton with onion domes, minarets and pinnacles that look as if they belong in India rather than on the English south coast. Inside, it is decorated in a lavish Chinese style. It is now a museum and one of Brighton's best-known landmarks.

## The main story

- **1787** – Henry Holland designs a neoclassical villa, the Marine Pavilion, for George, Prince of Wales, who visits Brighton for its sea air.
- **1815–1823** – George, now Prince Regent, has John Nash transform and greatly enlarge the building into the Indian-inspired palace seen today. Sources give the finish date as 1822 or 1823.
- **1820** – George becomes King George IV. He uses the Pavilion less as Brighton grows busier, and dies in 1830.
- **1830s–1840s** – William IV uses it. Queen Victoria visits but dislikes it, finding it too public, and chooses Osborne House on the Isle of Wight as the royal seaside home instead.
- **1850** – The building is sold to the town of Brighton. Much of the furniture and fittings had already been removed.
- **1914–1916** – Used as a military hospital for soldiers of the Indian Army wounded on the Western Front.
- **20th century onwards** – Restored and opened as a museum. Many original items have since been returned on loan from the Royal Collection.

## The alternative view

### An Indian palace on the English coast

The official story is that one prince's taste for the exotic produced an Indian-style palace in an English seaside town. In alternative history circles, the Pavilion is one of the best-known examples of "out of place" architecture: onion domes and minarets that match buildings across Russia, India and the Middle East. Was it a fashion, or a sign of a shared older building style later explained away as Regency fantasy?

### A rebuild of something earlier

Nash's palace was built around and over Holland's earlier Marine Pavilion. How much of what stands today is truly 1815–1823 work, and how much was there before? The real age of the structure under the domes is worth looking at.

### The tunnel

George IV had a tunnel built linking the Pavilion to the royal stables (now the Brighton Dome), reportedly so he could move about unseen. Tunnels and underground passages come up often in old world research. Where does it run, how was it built, and are there other passages under the site?

### Sold off and emptied

Within 30 years of its completion the palace was abandoned by the royal family, stripped of its contents and sold to the town. A short royal life, followed by a quick sale, is a pattern that appears with other grand buildings in this repo.

### What would support the alternative view

- Structure under the domes that predates Holland's 1787 villa
- Underground passages beyond the known tunnel
- Gaps in the building accounts for Nash's work

### What would weaken it

- Nash's published designs and the Royal Archives' building accounts
- Dated drawings and engravings of the building before, during and after the works
- Records of the tunnel's construction
