## Overview

All Saints is Marlow's parish church, standing beside the Thames at the end of the town's suspension bridge. Its tall spire is a landmark on the river. A church has stood here since the Middle Ages, but the old building decayed, part of its tower fell in 1831, and it was pulled down and rebuilt in 1832–35 to a Gothic design by Charles Frederick Inwood. Over the next 65 years the Victorian architect John Oldrid Scott added a chancel, remodelled the nave and rebuilt the tower and spire.

Inside are monuments brought from the old church, including the bust of Sir Miles Hobart, who died in 1632 and whose memorial was paid for by the House of Commons. The owner has found two things here that bear directly on this project's themes: a date inside the church written with a "J", and a chequerboard pattern on the outside, which the owner reads as a Masonic symbol.

## The main story

Marlow's medieval church stood on low ground by the river and suffered from flooding and decay by the early 19th century.

- **Middle Ages** – A parish church stands on the site by the Thames.
- **1632** – Sir Miles Hobart, MP for Marlow, who had been imprisoned for his part in the parliamentary protests of 1629, dies after a coaching accident on Holborn Hill in London. Sources give his date of death as 20 June or 4 July 1632.
- **1647** – A monument to Hobart, with his bust and a relief of the coach accident, is reported to have been paid for by the House of Commons.
- **1831** – The old church has decayed, and part of its tower falls (some sources say the spire collapsed). The same year, or the year after, William Tierney Clark's suspension bridge opens next to the church; Clark later built a larger version of it, the Chain Bridge in Budapest.
- **1832** – C. F. Inwood's design for a new church is shown at the Royal Academy.
- **1832–1835** – The old church is demolished and a new one built on the same site, of white brick in a Gothic style, with a tower and spire. Monuments from the old church, including Hobart's, are kept.
- **1835** – The new church is consecrated.
- **1875–1876** – A chancel is added by John Oldrid Scott (one record credits G. E. Street, who designed the pulpit).
- **1881–1882** – The nave is remodelled with six-bay arcades.
- **1889** – The nave is re-roofed.
- **1898–1899** – The upper part of the tower and the spire are rebuilt by Scott in stone and flint flushwork, to a design close to the earlier spire.
- **Today** – The church is in use and is listed Grade II*, in part for its monuments, especially Hobart's.

## Conservation

- All Saints is **Grade II*** listed (Historic England list entry 1332380). The churchyard wall and gate are listed separately.
- The church is recorded on the Church of England's Church Heritage Record (627813) and in Buckinghamshire's Historic Environment Record.

## The alternative view

The official account is of a medieval church that fell into ruin and was replaced by a Georgian-Gothic church, improved by the Victorians. Looked at from an "old world" perspective, several things invite a second look: a date inside written with a "J", a chequerboard pattern on the outside, the collapse of the old church just as a new bridge was built beside it, and a church whose fabric comes from three different campaigns. Each point below carries an evidence label: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative** (see the [research brief](https://github.com/martinv678/our-old-world/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

### A "J" date inside the church

**Reported** (owner's evidence; photograph to be added) – The owner has found a date inside the church written with a "J". Under the [letters in dates](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#letters-in-dates-the-j-argument) argument, this is exactly what the project is looking for: a year whose first figure is written as a letter rather than a "1", which on Fomenko's reading could stand for Jesus, putting the date about 1,000 years later than it appears.

The details to record from the owner's photograph:

- **Exactly how the date is written:** is the "J" (or "I") at the start of the year, in place of the "1", or at the end of a number in Roman numerals?
- **Where it is:** on which monument, tablet, brass or stone, and whether it came from the old church.
- **What the inscription says around it:** names, ages and other dates, which can be checked against parish registers.

Points to weigh up:

- **A final "j" in Roman numerals is normal.** In older writing, the last "i" of a number in Roman numerals was usually written as a "j", so "xiij" means 13 and "MDCxxxij" means 1632. This is a scribal habit and doesn't by itself change the date.
- **Old numerals could look like letters.** In 16th- and 17th-century carving and handwriting, the figure "1" was sometimes written in a form that looks like an "I" or "J". A leading "J" in a date in ordinary figures would need careful comparison with other numbers on the same inscription.
- **The monuments came from the old church.** Many of the older memorials, including Hobart's of 1632, were brought from the medieval building, so their dates belong to that church, not to the rebuilding of 1832–35.

**How to test it:** photograph the inscription straight on and in raking light; transcribe it letter by letter; compare the form of every number on it; and check the names and dates against the parish registers and against Cocks's transcriptions of the church's monuments (1890), which record how the dates were read at the time.

### A chequerboard on the side

**Reported** (owner's observation; photograph to be added) / **Speculative** (Masonic meaning) – The owner has found a chequerboard pattern on the outside of the church and reads it as a **Masonic** symbol: the black-and-white chequered floor, or "mosaic pavement", is one of the best-known symbols of Freemasonry, laid in the floors of Masonic lodges and representing the light and dark of human life.

Points to weigh up:

- **Flint and stone chequer is a traditional English decoration.** In flint-building areas such as the Chilterns and East Anglia, squares of knapped flint and pale stone were laid in chequer patterns ("flushwork") on medieval churches long before modern Freemasonry began in the early 18th century.
- **The tower and spire are flushwork of 1898–99.** Scott rebuilt the tower and spire in stone and flint flushwork, so the chequer may be part of that late Victorian work, copying the medieval tradition. Where exactly the pattern is (tower, walls, buttresses) will show which building campaign it belongs to.
- **Freemasons were often involved in church building.** Masonic lodges laid foundation stones with Masonic ceremony in the 18th and 19th centuries, and many architects, builders and patrons were Masons. If the church's records show Masonic involvement in 1832–35 or 1898–99, the chequer could carry a double meaning.

**How to test it:** record exactly where the chequer pattern is and what it's made of; check the dates of that part of the building; look in the parish records and local newspapers for any Masonic ceremony at the foundation stones of 1832 or 1898; and check whether Inwood, Scott or the main donors were Freemasons (the records of Marlow's Masonic lodge and the Library and Museum of Freemasonry in London can help).

### A collapse, and a new bridge beside it

**Documented** (dates) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – The old church's tower fell in 1831, just as the new suspension bridge was being finished beside it, and the church was rebuilt from 1832. In the "reset" theme, the sudden loss and replacement of an old building is worth noting, and the bridge works next to it raise the question of whether the building work on the riverbank affected the old church's foundations. The ordinary explanation is long decay and flooding on low ground by the river.

**How to test it:** find the reports of the collapse, the vestry minutes of 1831–32, and any record of complaints about the bridge works.

### Three churches in one

**Documented** – Today's church combines the white-brick nave of 1832–35, the chancel and arcades of the 1870s–80s, and the stone and flint tower and spire of 1898–99, with monuments from the medieval church inside. For dating any detail, including the "J" date and the chequer pattern, it's important to know which campaign it belongs to.

### Sound: bells and organ

**Speculative** (cymatics) – As a parish church, All Saints has bells and an organ, and Scott's tower carries the bells. For the [sound and cymatics](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#sound-and-cymatics-the-resonance-argument) theme, the number and pitch of the bells, the organ and any round windows should be recorded.

### Construction images

**Observed** (partial) – The rebuilding of 1832–35 came before photography, but Inwood's design drawings survive (one is in the RIBA collection, and his design was shown at the Royal Academy in 1832). Henry Taunt photographed Marlow in 1885, before the spire was rebuilt. No photographs of the 1898–99 rebuilding of the tower and spire have been found yet. See the [photo record](../../../ALTERNATIVE-VIEW.md#missing-construction-photographs-the-photo-record-argument).

### Timeline check

**What we know:** the church was rebuilt in about three years (1832–35), in white brick, with later additions in 1875–76, 1881–82, 1889 and 1898–99.

**The working:**

- **Time:** three years for a brick parish church is ordinary for the 1830s.
- **Delivery check:** Marlow is on the Thames, so brick, stone and timber could come by barge, and there were brickworks in the Chilterns around the town. The quantities haven't been found.

**Verdict for now:** **Plausible.** The building is modest and well recorded. The interest of this entry lies in its inscriptions and decoration: the "J" date and the chequer pattern.

### What would support the alternative view

- A "J" or "I" standing in place of the first "1" in a date in ordinary figures, on a monument whose other dates and names can be checked
- Evidence that the chequer pattern belongs to the medieval church or to a part of the building with recorded Masonic involvement
- Gaps or contradictions in the records of the old church's collapse and the rebuilding

### What would weaken it

- The "J" turning out to be the normal final "j" of a number in Roman numerals, or an old-style "1"
- Parish registers confirming the dates on the monument as written
- The chequer pattern dating from Scott's flushwork of 1898–99, with no Masonic involvement recorded
