# Castello di Sammezzano

| | |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Leccio, Reggello (FI), Tuscany, Italy |
| **Near** | Florence (approx. 30 km south-east) |
| **Built** | Early 1600s; remodelled c. 1853–1889 |
| **Architect** | Ferdinando Panciatichi Ximenes d'Aragona (owner and designer) |
| **Style** | Moorish Revival / Orientalist |
| **Original use** | Private residence |
| **Status** | Abandoned since 1990; closed to the public |

## Overview

Sammezzano is a hilltop castle in a large wooded park in the Valdarno, south-east of Florence. Behind a fairly sober exterior, its interiors are covered in brightly coloured Moorish-style tiles, stucco, arches and patterned ceilings, including the well-known *Sala dei Pavoni* (Hall of the Peacocks). It is often described as the largest example of Moorish Revival architecture in Italy.

## The main story

- **Early 1600s** – A villa is built on the site, later owned by the Ximenes d'Aragona family.
- **c. 1853–1889** – Marquis Ferdinando Panciatichi Ximenes d'Aragona transforms the building, designing the Moorish interiors himself and overseeing local craftsmen. Some sources give the dates as 1842–1890. He is said to have drawn on the Alhambra in Granada and other Islamic architecture, largely from books and drawings. He also planted the park with exotic trees, including giant sequoias.
- **1897** – Ferdinando dies. The estate passes down the family and later through several owners.
- **Post-war to 1990** – Used as a luxury hotel.
- **1990 onwards** – Empty and abandoned.
- **1999** – Bought at auction by a British-owned company. Restoration plans did not go ahead.
- **2012** – The Comitato FPXA, a volunteer group named after Ferdinando, is set up to protect and promote the castle.
- **Since then** – Repeated judicial auctions and proposed sales, none leading to restoration.

The castle is often said to have **365 rooms**, one for every day of the year. At least one source puts the real number of rooms at around 60.

## The alternative view

### One man, one lifetime

The official story says a single nobleman designed hundreds of square metres of intricate Moorish decoration himself, mostly from books, and had local Tuscan craftsmen carry it out in about 35 years. The quality and scale of the work raise a question: was it all made in the 19th century, or did Ferdinando restore and add to something that was already there?

### Moorish architecture in the middle of Tuscany

There is no Moorish building tradition in Tuscany. Supporters of the alternative view see Sammezzano as one of many "Orientalist" buildings that appeared across Europe in the 19th century (Villa Crespi, Rocchetta Mattei, the Brighton Pavilion and others) and ask whether these were a fashion, or the remains of a shared older style that was later relabelled as "revival".

### The 365 rooms

The "365 rooms, one for each day" story is repeated everywhere, but the actual count seems to be much lower. A building whose basic facts are unclear invites the question of what else in its story has been simplified or invented.

### Sealed and left to decay

The castle has been closed and empty since 1990, despite huge public interest, heritage campaigns and several buyers. Why has it proved so hard to open, restore or even survey such a famous building?

### Inscriptions and symbols

The interiors are full of inscriptions and symbols, including the phrase *"Non plus ultra"* ("nothing further beyond"). Many have not been fully catalogued or explained in English-language sources.

### What would support the alternative view

- Decoration or structure that clearly predates the 1850s
- A lack of invoices, workshop records or named craftsmen for the interiors
- Evidence of lower floors, rooms or tunnels not shown on known plans

### What would weaken it

- Ferdinando's surviving drawings, letters or accounts for the work
- Records of the craftsmen and workshops who made the tiles and stucco
- Dated photos or descriptions of the building before and during the remodelling
