## Overview

Villa Crespi stands just outside the old town of Orta San Giulio, above Lake Orta. Its tall minaret-style tower, horseshoe arches and stucco arabesques make it one of the best-known Orientalist buildings in northern Italy. It was first called **Villa Pia**, after the owner's wife, Pia Travelli.

## The main story

- **1879** – Cristoforo Benigno Crespi, a wealthy cotton industrialist, commissions the villa from the architect Angelo Colla. Crespi is said to have wanted it to recall buildings he had seen on business trips to the Middle East, and Baghdad in particular.
- **Late 19th century** – The villa becomes the family's lakeside residence. Crespi is also known for building the workers' village of Crespi d'Adda in Lombardy, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **1930s** – Reported to have hosted members of the House of Savoy.
- **Later 20th century** – Changes hands and is eventually turned into a hotel and restaurant, which it remains today.

## The alternative view

### A desert palace on an Alpine lake

A minaret overlooking a lake in the foothills of the Alps is striking. The official story puts it down to one industrialist's taste after his travels. The alternative view groups it with Sammezzano, Rocchetta Mattei and other Orientalist buildings across Europe, and asks whether this "revival" style was really new in the 19th century or a continuation of something older.

### How long did it take?

Most sources simply give 1879, but at least one says construction took around 30 years. That is a big difference. Which parts were finished when, and was there anything on the site before?

### Uncertain basic facts

Even the basics vary between sources: the architect is given as Angelo Colla or Angelo Costa, and Crespi's dates also differ between sources. As with other buildings in this project, gaps in the record are where the alternative questions begin.

### The tower

Towers, spires and minarets come up often in old world theories, sometimes linked to ideas about lost energy technology. What was the tower actually used for, and how is it built inside?

### What would support the alternative view

- Evidence of a building on the site before 1879
- Structural features that don't match an 1870s build
- Missing plans, invoices or construction photos

### What would weaken it

- Colla's original plans, or the Crespi family's building accounts
- Construction-period photos or newspaper reports
- Pre-1879 maps showing the site as empty
