## Overview

The Anhalter Bahnhof was once one of Berlin's grandest and busiest stations, the city's "gateway to the south", with trains to Dresden, Munich, Vienna and Rome. Today almost nothing is left. A lone fragment of its brick entrance portico stands on Askanischer Platz in front of an open park and the Tempodrom concert venue.

## The main story

- **1841** – The first Anhalter Bahnhof opens as the Berlin terminus of the Berlin–Anhalt railway.
- **1876–1880** – It's replaced by a much larger station designed by Franz Schwechten, chief architect of the railway company. The new station opens in 1880 in the presence of Emperor Wilhelm I. Its iron and glass train shed is among the largest in Europe at the time.
- **1880s–1930s** – One of Berlin's main long-distance stations, used by kings and heads of state arriving from the south.
- **1939** – An underground S-Bahn station opens nearby as part of the north–south tunnel.
- **1942–1945** – Used as a departure point for the deportation of Berlin's Jews to the Theresienstadt ghetto.
- **1943–1945** – Badly damaged in Allied bombing. A large air-raid bunker next to the station, built in 1943, survives today.
- **1952** – Rail services end. The station lies in West Berlin, but its lines are run by East Germany's railway, which diverts trains elsewhere.
- **1959–1960** – Despite protests, the remains are blown up and cleared, except for part of the entrance portico, which is kept as a memorial.

## The alternative view

### Erased rather than saved

The station survived the war damaged but still standing, and its front building was largely intact. It was then blown up in 1960, leaving just one fragment. The alternative view sees this as part of a pattern across Europe after the war: grand 19th-century buildings that could have been repaired were demolished instead. Was it only about cost and politics, or was something being removed?

### Built in four years

A huge brick and terracotta front building with one of the largest iron and glass halls in Europe was built in around four years, on the site of an older station. How much of the 1841 station's foundations or structure was reused?

### Tunnels and bunkers

The site is linked to the S-Bahn tunnel, and a massive concrete bunker still stands beside it. Old world research often asks what lies beneath stations like this. Are there tunnels, cellars or levels under the site that aren't on public plans?

### The surviving portico

The one fragment that was kept is the decorated entrance. What details does it preserve (sculpture, inscriptions, a clock), and what do they tell us about the building's original design and purpose?

### What would support the alternative view

- Evidence that the station's structure was sound and repairable before demolition
- Tunnels, cellars or structures under the site that aren't in published plans
- Structures older than 1841 found on the site

### What would weaken it

- Engineering surveys and city records explaining the decision to demolish
- Schwechten's drawings and the railway company's building records
- Archaeological or construction surveys of the site since 1960

## Open questions for contributors

- **Demolition dates:** Sources give 1959–1960, 1959–1961 or 1959–1962. Which is right?
- **The portico:** What sculptures and details survive on the fragment, and what did they represent?
- **Underground:** What is beneath the park and the Tempodrom today?
- **Why demolish?** What reasons did the West Berlin authorities give at the time?
- **Photos:** Historic photos and postcards from before 1900 are likely to be public domain.
