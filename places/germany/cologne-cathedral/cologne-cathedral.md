## Overview

Cologne Cathedral is a vast Gothic cathedral beside the Rhine, with two spires about 157 metres tall. Its construction is one of the strangest stories in architecture: building started in 1248, stopped for nearly 400 years with a medieval crane left standing on the unfinished south tower, and then resumed in 1842. It was finally finished in 1880, following medieval plans that had been rediscovered only a few decades earlier, one half of them in the attic of an inn.

## The main story

- **Roman period to 9th century** – The site lies inside the Roman city. Roman buildings, early churches and the Carolingian "Old Cathedral" stand here before the Gothic cathedral.
- **1248** – The Old Cathedral is damaged by fire and the foundation stone of the new Gothic cathedral is laid. It is built to house the relics of the Three Kings, brought to Cologne in 1164.
- **1322** – The choir is consecrated.
- **1473** – Work stops, with the choir finished and the south tower built only to the belfry level. A wooden crane stays on top of the tower for around 400 years.
- **1814** – A four-metre medieval parchment drawing of the west front is found in the attic of an inn in Darmstadt. The court architect Georg Moller identifies it as the left half of the façade plan.
- **1815 or 1816** – The other half is found in Paris and acquired by Sulpiz Boisserée, the campaigner for completing the cathedral. Together the halves form "Plan F", about 4 × 1.7 m on 20 sheets of parchment.
- **1842** – Work resumes, funded by the Prussian state and the new Central Cathedral Building Society (*Zentral-Dombau-Verein*), following the medieval plans but using modern methods, including an iron roof structure.
- **1868** – The medieval crane is finally removed from the south tower.
- **1880** – The last stone is set on the south spire in August, and the completion is celebrated on 15 October in the presence of Kaiser Wilhelm I. At about 157 m, the cathedral is often said to have been the tallest building in the world until the Washington Monument was finished in 1884.
- **1939–1945** – Hit many times in Allied bombing but remains standing.
- **1946–1997** – Excavations beneath the cathedral uncover Roman remains, earlier churches and Frankish royal graves (found in 1959).

## The alternative view

Each point below carries an evidence label: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative** (see the [research brief](https://github.com/martinv678/our-old-world/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

### 632 years, or something else?

**Speculative** – The official story is a medieval building left unfinished for four centuries, then completed in 38 years in the 19th century to the original design. The alternative view asks whether the medieval and 19th-century "halves" are really as distinct as the story says, or whether the dating of the different parts is less certain than claimed. If a large part of what is called "medieval" were in fact 19th-century, or the reverse, the whole timeline would shift.

### Plans rediscovered at the right time

**Documented** (discovery) / **Speculative** (interpretation) – Just as the movement to finish the cathedral was gathering pace, half of the medieval façade plan turned up in the attic of an inn in Darmstadt (1814), and the other half in Paris a year or two later, found by the very man campaigning for the completion. The alternative view asks:

- How did a 4-metre medieval drawing end up in an inn's attic, and how was the Paris half traced?
- How was each half proven to be medieval rather than a later drawing?
- Even the experts disagree on its date: Wikipedia gives the end of the 13th century, while the Metropolitan Museum dates a related plan to c. 1300–1350.

A convenient discovery is not proof of anything, but it is exactly the kind of moment where history can be shaped.

### Iron hidden in a Gothic shell

**Documented** – The 19th-century builders used an iron roof structure inside a Gothic exterior, matching medieval work on the outside with modern technology inside. The alternative view asks where else modern materials might be hidden behind "old" stone, and how easy it really is to tell 1250s stonework from 1850s stonework.

### The crane on the tower

**Reported** – A medieval wooden crane is said to have stood on the south tower for around 400 years, becoming a symbol of the city, before its removal in 1868. A timber crane surviving four centuries of weather on top of a tower is remarkable. Was it continuously maintained or replaced, and can its timber be dated?

### Fire clears the way

**Reported** – The Gothic cathedral began after the Old Cathedral was damaged by fire in 1248. Fires that clear the way for grand new buildings come up again and again in this project, from the Palace of Westminster to the Crystal Palace and Chicago's White City.

### Layers underneath

**Documented** – Excavations since 1946 have found Roman buildings with underfloor heating, Christian churches, the Carolingian Old Cathedral and the graves of a Merovingian noble family. The site has been rebuilt many times. The alternative view asks what the oldest layers really are, and whether anything in them doesn't fit the accepted sequence.

### Standing amid ruins

**Observed** – In 1945 the cathedral stood almost alone among the ruins of central Cologne. It was hit many times but did not fall. Was this down to its structure, to chance, or to it being used as a landmark by bomber crews, as is often said?

### Timeline check

**What we know:** between 1842 and 1880 the builders completed the nave, transepts, the west front and both 157 m towers, roughly 38 years of work, on top of the medieval choir and the base of the south tower.

**Comparisons:**

- [Saint Isaac's Cathedral](../../russia/saint-isaacs-cathedral/saint-isaacs-cathedral.md) took 40 years (1818–1858) with the resources of the Russian Empire.
- Ulm Minster, another medieval cathedral finished in the 19th century, had its spire completed in 1890 after work resumed in 1844.

**Working still needed:**

1. How much of the stonework was 19th-century versus medieval, by volume.
2. The size of the 19th-century workforce in the cathedral works (*Dombauhütte*).
3. Where the stone came from, and how it was quarried, cut and transported.
4. Annual spending, from the building society's and the Prussian state's accounts.

**Verdict for now:** **Plausible.** Thirty-eight years is a long build with state backing, steam power and railways, so the 19th-century completion isn't fast in itself. The real questions are about the earlier phases: what was actually standing in 1842, and how securely its parts are dated.

### What would support the alternative view

- Construction evidence that doesn't match the official medieval and 19th-century phases
- Doubts about the age or origin of the rediscovered plans
- Structures beneath the cathedral that predate 1248 and don't fit the known sequence of earlier churches
- Stone or timber dating that contradicts the official phases

### What would weaken it

- The cathedral works' (Dombauhütte) records from 1842 onwards
- Scientific dating of the stonework, mortar and timber, including the medieval roof and any crane timbers
- The published excavation reports
- Pre-1842 drawings and descriptions showing exactly what was standing

## Open questions for contributors

- **Completion date:** The last stone was set in August 1880 and the completion was celebrated on 15 October 1880. Can both dates be confirmed from records?
- **The plans:** How exactly did half of Plan F end up in a Darmstadt inn's attic, and how was the Paris half traced?
- **Plan dating:** On what evidence is Plan F dated to around 1280–1300?
- **The crane:** Was it the same crane for 400 years? Has its timber ever been dated?
- **Tallest building:** Was it really the tallest building in the world from 1880 to 1884?
- **Underground:** What do the excavation reports show for the oldest layers?
- **Photos:** Photos from before 1880, with the crane on the tower, would be especially useful.

## Leads to follow up

- **Cologne Cathedral archive and works (*Dombauarchiv* and *Dombauhütte*):** records of the 19th-century building campaign; the original Plan F is kept by the cathedral works.
- **Sulpiz Boisserée's book** *Geschichte und Beschreibung des Doms von Köln* (1821–31), which published the plans and is the earliest detailed account.
- **Georg Moller's papers** in Darmstadt, on the 1814 attic discovery.
- **Excavation reports:** including the University of Bamberg study of finds from the first building phase (1248–1322).
- **Dendrochronology:** any tree-ring dating of the medieval roof timbers or the crane.
- **Period newspapers:** reports of the 1842 restart and the 1880 completion. The Illustrated London News covered the cathedral (via the Victorian Web).
- **Old photographs:** Cologne's photo archive (*Rheinisches Bildarchiv*) and Wikimedia Commons for photos with the crane on the tower.
