## Overview

Under the streets of Pioneer Square, Seattle's oldest neighbourhood, lies a hidden layer of old sidewalks and shopfronts. After a great fire in 1889, the city raised its streets by one to two storeys, leaving the original ground floors and sidewalks below the new street level. Parts of this "underground" can be visited today, and purple glass skylights in the pavement still light the passages beneath.

## The main story

- **Before 1889** – Pioneer Square is built largely on filled-in tidelands. Streets flood regularly, and drainage is poor.
- **6 June 1889** – The Great Seattle Fire destroys the business district.
- **1889 onwards** – The city rebuilds in brick and stone, and raises the street level by about 12 to 30 feet to fix flooding and drainage. Buildings are put up before the streets are raised, so their original ground floors end up below the new streets. Sidewalks are covered over, turning them into tunnels, with glass prism skylights set into the new pavements.
- **Early 1900s** – The underground passages fall out of use and are closed off.
- **1960s onwards** – Parts are reopened as guided tours.

## The alternative view

### The classic "mud flood" case

Buried ground floors, doorways and shopfronts below street level are the central evidence for mud flood theories, which suggest a sudden worldwide event buried earlier city levels. Seattle is one of the most famous examples. The official story is that the streets were deliberately raised. The alternative view asks whether a raising project fully explains what is found, and why so many cities around the world have similar buried levels.

### Why build first and raise later?

The official explanation is that building owners rebuilt straight after the fire, before the streets were raised. But building shopfronts and entrances that would soon be buried seems odd. Were some of these lower levels already below ground level before 1889?

### Many cities, one pattern

Similar raised streets and buried storeys are reported in Chicago (which raised its streets in the 1850s–60s), Sacramento and other cities. Does each have its own local explanation, or is there a common cause?

### What would support the alternative view

- Evidence that lower floors were already below ground before the regrading
- Similar buried levels in places with no recorded raising project
- Fill material that doesn't match deliberate engineering works

### What would weaken it

- City records of the regrading works, contracts and dates
- Photographs showing the streets before, during and after raising
- Building permits showing the original street-level entrances

## Open questions for contributors

- **How high?** How much were individual streets raised: 12 feet, 30 feet, or one to two storeys?
- **The fire:** How many blocks did it destroy? Sources give 25 to 33.
- **Closure:** When and why were the underground passages closed?
- **Other cities:** Which other cities raised their streets, and how do the records compare?
- **Photos:** Photos from 1889–1900 showing the raising would be especially useful.
