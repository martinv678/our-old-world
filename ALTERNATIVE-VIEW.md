# The alternative view

This page explains the ideas behind the "alternative view" sections in this project: where they come from, what they say, how they apply to the buildings here, and who to watch and read to go deeper.

## Where the ideas come from

- **Tartary on old maps.** From the 1500s to the 1800s, many European maps labelled a huge area of northern and central Asia as *Tartaria* or *Great Tartary*. In mainstream history this was a loose name for lands Europeans knew little about, inhabited by Tatar, Mongol and Turkic peoples. The alternative view reads it as evidence of a forgotten empire.
- **New Chronology (1970s–1980s onwards).** Russian mathematician Anatoly Fomenko and his co-author Gleb Nosovsky argued that accepted world history is badly mis-dated and that much of it was compressed or invented. Many later "Tartaria" ideas build on this.
- **Nikolai Levashov.** A Russian writer who popularised the idea of a "Great Tartaria" as a lost civilisation in the 2000s.
- **The English-speaking internet (about 2016–2018 onwards).** The ideas spread through Reddit forums and YouTube channels, combining Tartaria with "mud flood" theories and a focus on grand 19th-century buildings.

## Satan's Little Season

### The idea

Revelation 20 describes Satan being bound for a thousand years, after which "he must be loosed a little season" (Revelation 20:3, King James Version) before the final judgement. Most Christian traditions place the thousand years either in the future or treat them as symbolic.

The "Satan's Little Season" view says the thousand-year reign has **already happened** in the past, and that we are living now in the little season of deception that follows. Supporters connect this with:

- **Tartaria:** the thousand years as a golden age, whose civilisation and buildings were inherited and re-labelled
- **the reset and mud flood:** catastrophe and the loss of that world, sometimes linked to Satan's release
- **altered timelines and "missing centuries"**, and sometimes the Mandela Effect

### How it applies to this project

It is a framework for the whole story rather than a claim about any one building. For each building it raises the question: does the building belong to an older age that was later claimed, and do its records show signs of being rewritten?

### What critics say

- **Mainstream Christian commentators** read the little season as a short final rebellion still in the future, or as a symbol, not a historical period.
- **A detailed review** of one version of the theory argues that its order of events conflicts with the sequence in Revelation 20 itself, and calls it a synthetic chronology rather than something the text sets out.
- Some Reformed writers do link the little season to deception in the present age, so the idea of living in it isn't entirely new, even if the Tartaria connection is.

## Letters in dates (the "J" argument)

### The idea

This comes from Anatoly Fomenko's *New Chronology* (volume 1, chapter 6). The argument is that in medieval documents years were written with the name of Jesus in front, as a letter **I** (or sometimes **J**) or **X**, for example "I.255" meaning "year 255 of Jesus". Later readers took the I or J for the number 1 and read it as "1255". On this reading, many dates have been made about **1,000 years older** than they really are.

Supporters also point out that **J** only became a separate letter quite late:

- Gian Giorgio Trissino first formally distinguished I and J as separate letters in **1524**.
- In English, the distinction only settled in the **1600s**. The first English book to explain it is usually dated to 1633.

So, the argument goes, a date stone or document that uses a "J" in a way that looks old should make us ask when it was really made.

### How it applies to this project

Look closely at **date stones, foundation stones, inscriptions, coins and old documents** connected with each building:

- Is the year written with a letter in front of it, or a "1" that looks like an I or J?
- Does a "J" appear in an inscription supposedly older than the 1500s?
- Do the dates on the building match the dates in the records?

Record any examples in the entry's **Anomaly log**, with a photo if possible.

### What critics say

- **J as a numeral is documented.** In Roman numerals, a final *i* was often written as *j*, so 13 might be written "xiij" and 23 "XXIIJ". A "j" in a number is therefore expected in older documents and doesn't by itself mean the date is wrong.
- **Lowercase i and j were already different shapes** before 1524; only the capital letter shared one form. So the presence of a "j" doesn't automatically date a text to after 1524.
- **Dating doesn't rest on written dates alone.** Historians also use tree-ring dating, radiocarbon, astronomical records (eclipses) and independent manuscript traditions, which would all need to be wrong in the same way.
- Mainstream historians reject the New Chronology as a whole.

The useful test for this project is concrete: find actual inscriptions and documents and look at exactly how their dates are written.

## Materials and delivery (the logistics argument)

### The idea

This is the owner's own argument. It starts from the materials rather than the buildings. The grand buildings in this project used millions of bricks, thousands of tons of stone and iron, and huge amounts of timber and glass. In the official story, all of this was made, carried and put in place with horses, carts, barges and early railways. And many of these buildings were going up at the same time, in the same cities, alongside new railways, docks, sewers and whole districts of housing. On this view, the whole country would have had to be one constant building site, and the technology and transport of the time don't seem equal to it. If the logistics don't add up, that would suggest some of these buildings were already standing.

### How it applies to this project

For each building, the **Timeline check** should include a **delivery check**: how much material was needed, where it came from, how it was carried, and how many loads a day that means. Then add up what was being built in the same city at the same time. The working below gives the method and the period figures to use.

**Period figures** (approximate; see Sources):

- **A brick** weighed about 3 kg. A one-horse dray was customarily loaded with about **500 bricks**, about 1.5 tonnes.
- **Britain made about 1.7 billion bricks in 1840**, according to the brick tax returns (the tax was charged per thousand bricks from 1784 to 1850, so output was counted). Output grew a great deal after the tax was abolished.
- **Bricks for London** came largely by **Thames sailing barge** from brickfields in Kent and Essex, and from brickfields around the edge of the city. Northern cities like Manchester had their own brickworks and canals as well as railways.
- **London had more than 300,000 horses** pulling vehicles in 1893, according to W. J. Gordon's *The Horse World of London*.

**Worked examples:**

| Building | Bricks | Years of building | Bricks a day (300 working days a year) | One-horse loads a day (500 bricks) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Royal Albert Hall](places/uk/royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md) | about 6 million | about 3 | about 6,700 | about 13 |
| [Manchester Town Hall](places/uk/manchester-town-hall/manchester-town-hall.md) | more than 14 million | about 7 | about 6,700 | about 13 |
| [Royal Courts of Justice](places/uk/royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md) | about 35 million (not confirmed) | about 8 | about 14,600 | about 29 |

If each cart made three or four round trips a day from a wharf, rail yard or brickfield a mile or two away, about 13 loads a day needs only four or five carts. For a single building, the delivery side is small: the Albert Hall's whole yearly need (about 2 million bricks) is about a tenth of one per cent of Britain's 1840 output.

**The bigger question is the total.** Britain's 1.7 billion bricks a year in 1840 works out at about 5.7 million bricks every working day, or more than 11,000 one-horse loads a day if all of it went by road, and output rose after 1850. So the owner's picture of a constant building site everywhere is, in a sense, what the records show: Victorian Britain really was building on an enormous scale. The test is whether the recorded capacity (brickworks, quarries, ironworks, barges, canals, railways, horses and workers) matches everything that was built. That has to be checked across a whole city or region, not one building at a time.

### What critics say

- **The numbers per building are modest.** The worked examples above need a handful of carts and a few dozen workers, not a vast operation.
- **Much of the transport was by water.** A barge or canal boat carried many cart-loads at once, and most brick traffic only went by road for the last mile or two.
- **The output was counted.** The brick tax returns, quarry and ironworks records, canal and railway goods traffic, and contractors' accounts all record production and delivery.
- **The building boom is well documented.** London's population grew from about 1 million in 1801 to about 6.5 million in 1901, and the houses, railways and sewers built for them are recorded in maps, Acts of Parliament and photographs.

### How to test it

- For each building, find the **contractors' accounts** and the names of the **brickmakers, quarries and ironworks** that supplied it.
- Compare the **total material needed** for a city's major buildings in a decade with the **recorded output** of its brickworks and quarries (brick tax returns up to 1850, trade directories and Board of Trade figures after).
- Look for **photographs and engravings** of brickfields, kilns, barges, goods yards and building sites. Where a big building has none (see the [Royal Courts of Justice](places/uk/royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md#no-construction-photographs-found)), note it.
- Check the **transport records**: canal and river tolls (for example, tolls charged per thousand bricks on barges), railway goods traffic and local carting firms.

If the recorded production and transport capacity falls clearly short of what was built, that would support the argument. If it matches, it would weaken it.

## How to weigh what you watch

- **Note the claim, then find the source.** If a video says a building went up in a year, look for the records: newspapers, contracts, photos.
- **Label it.** Use the project's evidence labels: **Documented**, **Observed**, **Reported** or **Speculative**.
- **Look for the ordinary explanation too.** For example, the "Ouverture 1911" caption on [Villa Sultana](places/italy/villa-sultana/villa-sultana.md) might mean the start of a casino season. Testing both readings is what makes a finding stand up.
- **Bring leads back here.** Add them to an entry's **Research notes** section, or to the [research list](links/research-list.md), so they can be followed up.
