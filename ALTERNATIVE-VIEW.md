# The alternative view

This page explains the ideas behind the "alternative view" sections in this project: where they come from, what they say, how they apply to the buildings here, and who to watch and read to go deeper.

## Where the ideas come from

- **Tartary on old maps.** From the 1500s to the 1800s, many European maps labelled a huge area of northern and central Asia as *Tartaria* or *Great Tartary*. In mainstream history this was a loose name for lands Europeans knew little about, inhabited by Tatar, Mongol and Turkic peoples. The alternative view reads it as evidence of a forgotten empire.
- **New Chronology (1970s–1980s onwards).** Russian mathematician Anatoly Fomenko and his co-author Gleb Nosovsky argued that accepted world history is badly mis-dated and that much of it was compressed or invented. Many later "Tartaria" ideas build on this.
- **Nikolai Levashov.** A Russian writer who popularised the idea of a "Great Tartaria" as a lost civilisation in the 2000s.
- **The English-speaking internet (about 2016–2018 onwards).** The ideas spread through Reddit forums and YouTube channels, combining Tartaria with "mud flood" theories and a focus on grand 19th-century buildings.

## Satan's Little Season

### The idea

Revelation 20 describes Satan being bound for a thousand years, after which "he must be loosed a little season" (Revelation 20:3, King James Version) before the final judgement. Most Christian traditions place the thousand years either in the future or treat them as symbolic.

The "Satan's Little Season" view says the thousand-year reign has **already happened** in the past, and that we are living now in the little season of deception that follows. Supporters connect this with:

- **Tartaria:** the thousand years as a golden age, whose civilisation and buildings were inherited and re-labelled
- **the reset and mud flood:** catastrophe and the loss of that world, sometimes linked to Satan's release
- **altered timelines and "missing centuries"**, and sometimes the Mandela Effect

### How it applies to this project

It is a framework for the whole story rather than a claim about any one building. For each building it raises the question: does the building belong to an older age that was later claimed, and do its records show signs of being rewritten?

### What critics say

- **Mainstream Christian commentators** read the little season as a short final rebellion still in the future, or as a symbol, not a historical period.
- **A detailed review** of one version of the theory argues that its order of events conflicts with the sequence in Revelation 20 itself, and calls it a synthetic chronology rather than something the text sets out.
- Some Reformed writers do link the little season to deception in the present age, so the idea of living in it isn't entirely new, even if the Tartaria connection is.

## Letters in dates (the "J" argument)

### The idea

This comes from Anatoly Fomenko's *New Chronology* (volume 1, chapter 6). The argument is that in medieval documents years were written with the name of Jesus in front, as a letter **I** (or sometimes **J**) or **X**, for example "I.255" meaning "year 255 of Jesus". Later readers took the I or J for the number 1 and read it as "1255". On this reading, many dates have been made about **1,000 years older** than they really are.

Supporters also point out that **J** only became a separate letter quite late:

- Gian Giorgio Trissino first formally distinguished I and J as separate letters in **1524**.
- In English, the distinction only settled in the **1600s**. The first English book to explain it is usually dated to 1633.

So, the argument goes, a date stone or document that uses a "J" in a way that looks old should make us ask when it was really made.

### How it applies to this project

Look closely at **date stones, foundation stones, inscriptions, coins and old documents** connected with each building:

- Is the year written with a letter in front of it, or a "1" that looks like an I or J?
- Does a "J" appear in an inscription supposedly older than the 1500s?
- Do the dates on the building match the dates in the records?

Record any examples in the entry's **Anomaly log**, with a photo if possible.

### What critics say

- **J as a numeral is documented.** In Roman numerals, a final *i* was often written as *j*, so 13 might be written "xiij" and 23 "XXIIJ". A "j" in a number is therefore expected in older documents and doesn't by itself mean the date is wrong.
- **Lowercase i and j were already different shapes** before 1524; only the capital letter shared one form. So the presence of a "j" doesn't automatically date a text to after 1524.
- **Dating doesn't rest on written dates alone.** Historians also use tree-ring dating, radiocarbon, astronomical records (eclipses) and independent manuscript traditions, which would all need to be wrong in the same way.
- Mainstream historians reject the New Chronology as a whole.

The useful test for this project is concrete: find actual inscriptions and documents and look at exactly how their dates are written.

## Materials and delivery (the logistics argument)

### The idea

This is the owner's own argument. It starts from the materials rather than the buildings. The grand buildings in this project used millions of bricks, thousands of tons of stone and iron, and huge amounts of timber and glass. In the official story, all of this was made, carried and put in place with horses, carts, barges and early railways. And many of these buildings were going up at the same time, in the same cities, alongside new railways, docks, sewers and whole districts of housing. On this view, the whole country would have had to be one constant building site, and the technology and transport of the time don't seem equal to it. If the logistics don't add up, that would suggest some of these buildings were already standing.

### How it applies to this project

For each building, the **Timeline check** should include a **delivery check**: how much material was needed, where it came from, how it was carried, and how many loads a day that means. Then add up what was being built in the same city at the same time. The working below gives the method and the period figures to use.

**Period figures** (approximate; see Sources):

- **A brick** weighed about 3–3.5 kg (a Victorian imperial brick of about 9 × 4½ × 3 inches is roughly 2 litres of fired clay), so 500 bricks weigh about 1.5–1.75 tonnes.
- **A one-horse load** is uncertain. One local history source says a one-horse dray was customarily loaded with about **500 bricks** ("over 1,500 kg"), but another period rule of thumb is about **one tonne per horse**, which would mean about **300 bricks**. The working below shows both. A period source for cart loads is still needed.
- **Britain made about 1.7 billion bricks in 1840**, according to the brick tax returns (the tax was charged per thousand bricks from 1784 to 1850, so output was counted). Output grew a great deal after the tax was abolished.
- **Bricks for London** came largely by **Thames sailing barge** from brickfields in Kent and Essex, and from brickfields around the edge of the city. Northern cities like Manchester had their own brickworks and canals as well as railways.
- **London had more than 300,000 horses** pulling vehicles in 1893, according to W. J. Gordon's *The Horse World of London*.

**Worked examples:**

| Building | Bricks | Years of building | Bricks a day (300 working days a year) | One-horse loads a day (500 bricks) | One-horse loads a day (300 bricks) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Royal Albert Hall](places/uk/royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md) | about 6 million | about 3 | about 6,700 | about 13 | about 22 |
| [Manchester Town Hall](places/uk/manchester-town-hall/manchester-town-hall.md) | more than 14 million | about 7 | about 6,700 | about 13 | about 22 |
| [Royal Courts of Justice](places/uk/royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md) | about 35 million (not confirmed) | about 8 | about 14,600 | about 29 | about 49 |

If each cart made three or four round trips a day from a wharf, rail yard or brickfield a mile or two away, about 13 to 22 loads a day needs only four to seven carts. For a single building, the delivery side is small: the Albert Hall's whole yearly need (about 2 million bricks) is about a tenth of one per cent of Britain's 1840 output.

**The bigger question is the total.** The great public buildings were only a small part of what was being built. Most bricks went into ordinary houses, along with shops, pubs, theatres, chapels, schools, warehouses, railway viaducts and sewers. So the test has to be done for a whole city, everything included.

#### A whole city: London in 1879

*The Builder* estimated in 1879, when brickmaking was at or near its peak, that **London used about 700 million bricks a year**. By the late 1890s, Greater London's demand is put at about **1,470 million a year**. Almost half of London's bricks around 1880 are said to have come from east Kent, by barge. These figures already include the houses, theatres and everything else.

| | London, 1879 | Greater London, late 1890s |
|---|---|---|
| Bricks a year | about 700 million | about 1,470 million |
| Bricks a working day (300 days) | about 2.3 million | about 4.9 million |
| Weight a day (about 3 kg a brick) | about 7,000 tonnes | about 14,700 tonnes |
| One-horse loads a day at 500 bricks, if all went by road | about 4,700 | about 9,800 |
| One-horse loads a day at 300 bricks, if all went by road | about 7,800 | about 16,300 |
| Carts needed, at three trips a day each | about 1,600–2,600 | about 3,300–5,400 |
| Bricklayers laying at once, at 500–1,000 bricks a day each | about 2,300–4,700 | about 4,900–9,800 |

How this compares with what London had:

- **Horses:** about 1,600–2,600 carts in 1879, or about 3,300–5,400 in the late 1890s (depending on the load size), would be about 0.5–2% of the more than 300,000 horses working in London in 1893.
- **Barges:** if about half the bricks (350 million a year in 1879) came up the Thames from Kent, and a typical sailing barge carried about 120 tons (sources give 80–150), about 40,000 bricks a load, that's about 8,750 barge-loads a year, or about 29 a day. There were about 2,000 Thames sailing barges working around 1900 (one source says 4,000), so each would have needed to carry bricks only four or five times a year.
- **The great buildings' share:** the Royal Courts of Justice, at about 4.4 million bricks a year if the 35 million figure is right, would have been about 0.6% of London's annual use. The Royal Albert Hall, at about 2 million a year, about 0.3%.
- **Bricklayers:** London would have needed several thousand bricklayers laying at once, with labourers, scaffolders and carters on top. The census occupation tables for 1871, 1881 and 1891 give the real numbers of bricklayers in London, and should be checked against this.

So, on the period figures found so far, the city-wide demand, houses included, could be carried by a small share of London's horses and barges. The owner's point stands, though: this is only the bricks. A full check also needs:

- **stone, timber, iron, glass, slate, lime, cement and sand**, which all had to be quarried, made and carried too
- **the brickmaking itself**: the brickfields, clay, kilns and fuel (London stock bricks were fired in clamps with ash and household refuse mixed into the clay), and the workers to make 700 million bricks a year
- **fodder and stabling** for the horses, and the roads, wharves and goods yards to handle it all
- **the railways, docks and sewers** that were being built at the same time and used materials of their own

### What critics say

- **The numbers per building are modest.** The worked examples above need a handful of carts and a few dozen workers, not a vast operation.
- **Much of the transport was by water.** A barge or canal boat carried many cart-loads at once, and most brick traffic only went by road for the last mile or two.
- **The output was counted.** The brick tax returns, quarry and ironworks records, canal and railway goods traffic, and contractors' accounts all record production and delivery.
- **The building boom is well documented.** London's population grew from about 1 million in 1801 to about 6.5 million in 1901, and the houses, railways and sewers built for them are recorded in maps, Acts of Parliament and photographs.

### How to test it

- For each building, find the **contractors' accounts** and the names of the **brickmakers, quarries and ironworks** that supplied it.
- Find the number of **bricklayers, labourers and carters** in each city in the census occupation tables (1871, 1881, 1891), and compare it with the workforce the city-wide brick figures need.
- Repeat the city-wide working for **stone, timber and iron**, not just bricks.
- Compare the **total material needed** for a city's major buildings in a decade with the **recorded output** of its brickworks and quarries (brick tax returns up to 1850, trade directories and Board of Trade figures after).
- Look for **photographs and engravings** of brickfields, kilns, barges, goods yards and building sites. Where a big building has none (see the [Royal Courts of Justice](places/uk/royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md#no-construction-photographs-found)), note it.
- Check the **transport records**: canal and river tolls (for example, tolls charged per thousand bricks on barges), railway goods traffic and local carting firms.

If the recorded production and transport capacity falls clearly short of what was built, that would support the argument. If it matches, it would weaken it.

## Skill and precision (the master builders argument)

### The idea

This is also the owner's own argument. Buildings like the Royal Courts of Justice, Manchester Town Hall and the Royal Albert Hall needed vaults, towers, carved stone and iron domes, all made to fit exactly. The people who built them would have had to be masters of their craft, in very large numbers, and to get it right first time, with no computers, no modern surveying instruments and no second chances on a wall of cut stone. If there weren't enough skilled people, or if the work is more precise than the methods of the time allowed, that would suggest the buildings, or parts of them, came from somewhere else.

### How it applies to this project

For each building, ask:

- **How many skilled trades were needed**, and where did the masons, carvers, bricklayers and ironworkers come from?
- **Was it right first time?** Look for evidence of mistakes, rebuilding, changes of design during construction, collapses, repairs and complaints. A building that was built in one go with no recorded problems is more striking than one with a long list of corrections.
- **How was the precision achieved?** Look for drawings, templates, models, trial assemblies and specialist firms.

### What the entries show so far

The buildings in this project have a mixed record, which is useful evidence in itself:

- **Not always right first time.** The [Royal Albert Hall](places/uk/royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md#a-hall-that-echoed-from-the-first-day) had a bad echo from its opening day that wasn't fixed until 1969. [Alexandra Palace](places/uk/alexandra-palace/alexandra-palace.md) burned down 16 days after it opened and was rebuilt to a different design. The [Natural History Museum](places/uk/natural-history-museum/natural-history-museum.md) and the [Royal Courts of Justice](places/uk/royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md) were redesigned and cut back to save money. The Tay Bridge in Scotland collapsed in a storm on 28 December 1879, taking a train with it.
- **Precision was planned for.** The Albert Hall's iron dome was [assembled once in Manchester](places/uk/royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md#the-dome-built-twice) before being taken apart and rebuilt in London, to make sure it fitted. Terracotta for the Albert Hall and the Natural History Museum was made in moulds in a factory, so repeated detail didn't depend on each carver's hand.
- **Skilled workers were scarce.** When the masons at the [Royal Courts of Justice](places/uk/royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md#who-really-built-it) went on strike, the contractors brought in masons from abroad, mostly from Germany. That suggests skilled stonemasons were in short supply and in demand. It cuts both ways: it supports the idea that skill was a real constraint, and shows that the gap was filled from elsewhere.
- **Large numbers of drawings survive.** The architects of these buildings, such as Street and Waterhouse, left thousands of drawings, which show the designs being worked out and changed.

### What critics say

- **The skills were taught and recorded.** Trades were learned through apprenticeships, typically of several years, and the methods are written down in Victorian builders' manuals, pattern books and journals such as *The Builder*.
- **Victorian architects studied medieval buildings closely.** The Gothic Revival was based on measured drawings of real medieval churches and halls, which showed how to build vaults, arches and towers.
- **Mistakes did happen.** Collapses, fires, faulty acoustics, leaking roofs and redesigns are well recorded for buildings of this period.

### How to test it

- Use the **census occupation tables** (1851–1901) to find how many masons, carvers, bricklayers and other building tradesmen there were in each city, and compare them with what was being built.
- Look for **records of mistakes**: rebuilt walls, failed foundations, design changes during building, disputes with contractors and repair bills soon after completion.
- Look for the **tools and methods**: templates, setting-out drawings, full-size details and trial assemblies in the architects' and contractors' records.
- Check **the quality of the work itself**: is the stonework and detailing as uniform and precise as the claimed methods would allow, or more so?

## Missing construction photographs (the photo record argument)

### The idea

This is the owner's own argument. Photography was well established by the 1850s, yet for some of the great 19th-century buildings no photographs of them being built have been found. And the buildings with no construction photographs tend to be the ones that look like buildings from much older periods: Gothic halls and towers in the style of the 13th century, Romanesque churches of the 11th and 12th, classical temples. If a building that looks medieval has no record of being built in the 1870s, perhaps it wasn't built in the 1870s at all, but is older than the official story says, and was completed, refaced or simply claimed.

### How it applies to this project

For every building, the **Records audit** should say whether construction photographs or engravings have been found, and the building should be added to the table below. Once enough buildings are in the table, it will show whether the missing photographs really go with the older-looking styles, or with something else, such as the date of building or the kind of project.

- **Totals:** 7 buildings with construction photographs (Cologne, the Crystal Palace, the Galleria, the St Pancras train shed, the Albert Hall, Tower Bridge and Chicago's Palace of Fine Arts), 2 with some (Westminster and the Natural History Museum), 2 possible (the Trocadéro and Dresden), 2 with lithographs or engravings only (Saint Isaac's and Melbourne), 2 from before photography (Palmanova and the Royal Pavilion), and 11 with none found.
- **Iron and engineering were photographed; stone and brick often weren't.** The clearest pattern is not about style but about what was being built. Iron and glass structures, bridges and world's fair buildings were photographed while going up: the Crystal Palace, the Galleria's iron frame, the St Pancras train shed, the Albert Hall dome, Tower Bridge's steel and the Chicago fair. At **St Pancras**, the iron train shed was photographed but no photograph of the Gothic hotel going up has been found, even though they were built side by side.
- **The owner's pattern partly holds.** The buildings with no construction photographs found include the Gothic civic buildings (Manchester Town Hall, Royal Courts of Justice), the St Pancras hotel, and the Moorish and eclectic villas and castles (Sammezzano, Rocchetta Mattei, Villa Crespi, Villa Sultana). These are the buildings that look most like older periods.
- **But there are exceptions both ways.** Cologne Cathedral and the Palace of Westminster, both Gothic, were photographed, and so was the Natural History Museum's site. Tower Bridge was designed to look medieval and was photographed in detail. And some buildings in newer styles, such as the German stations, haven't turned up construction photographs either.
- **Who paid matters.** The photographed buildings were mostly big public or engineering showpieces with official photographers (the city of Milan, the Chicago fair, the Crystal Palace Company, the Albert Hall). The private villas and castles were built for individuals who may never have commissioned photographs.

So far the evidence fits the critics' explanation (engineering showpieces were photographed, other buildings often weren't) at least as well as the owner's (older-looking buildings have no construction record). The St Pancras hotel, Manchester Town Hall and the Royal Courts of Justice are the best test cases: if a proper archive search still finds nothing for any of them, while their iron and engineering neighbours were photographed, that would strengthen the owner's argument (**Speculative**).

### What critics say

- **Construction photography was occasional before the 1880s.** Long exposure times made busy building sites hard to photograph, and workers often appear only by accident. Photographing building works became common with public engineering projects and contractors' records in the 1880s–1900s, and routine after about 1890. So the earlier a building, the less likely it is to have been photographed while being built.
- **Engineering projects were photographed more.** Bridges, iron halls and domes, such as the Brooklyn Bridge (from 1869–70), the Crystal Palace, the Albert Hall dome and Tower Bridge, were showpieces of engineering, and their builders wanted a record. A council or a government office building a stone hall may not have commissioned one.
- **Engravings were the usual record.** Journals like *The Builder* and the *Illustrated London News* recorded progress in engravings, which have to be searched as well as photographs.
- **Not online isn't the same as missing.** Many early photographs survive only as prints in archives that haven't been put online.
- **Older buildings had to be cleared first.** At the Royal Courts of Justice, about 450 houses were demolished to make the site, which Parliament debated and paid for. If the old maps show those streets on the site, a much older building couldn't have been standing there.

### How to test it

- **Complete the table** for every entry, giving the earliest construction image found and where it is held, or "none found" with the archives searched.
- **Compare by style, date and type**: do the gaps follow style (Gothic, Romanesque, classical), date (before or after about 1880) or type of project (engineering or civic)?
- For each "none found", search the **national and local archives**, the architects' and contractors' records, and the engravings in *The Builder*, *The Building News* and the *Illustrated London News*.
- **Check the site before the build** on old maps: was it empty, or covered by other buildings that were demolished?
- **Look for physical dating evidence** in the building: machine-made bricks, Portland cement mortar, rolled iron and steel, and tree-ring dating of roof timbers would all show when it was really built.

## Sound and cymatics (the resonance argument)

### The idea

This is the owner's own view. Many of these buildings, especially churches and cathedrals with rose windows, were designed with **cymatics** in mind: the patterns that sound makes when it vibrates a surface. On this view, the builders understood which sounds and acoustics are best for the human body (**Speculative**: no direct evidence has been found yet). Rose windows exist because they match the patterns that sound creates, and organs and bells were used to turn the whole building into an instrument.

### How it applies to this project

For each building, record its **sound features** and look for links between its shapes and sound:

- **Rose windows:** how many divisions (petals, spokes, rings), and when they were made
- **Organs and bells:** who made them, how big they are, and what notes the bells are tuned to
- **Domes, vaults and towers:** what the space sounds like, and whether its resonance has been measured
- **Any written evidence** that the builders thought about sound when designing the building

### What the evidence shows so far

- **The patterns are real.** When a plate covered in sand is made to vibrate, the sand gathers into patterns along the lines that don't move. Robert Hooke described this in 1680, and Ernst Chladni studied it systematically in 1787, so the patterns are called **Chladni figures**. Hans Jenny coined the word **cymatics** in 1967. On a round plate, the patterns are made of lines radiating from the centre and rings around it, which is also the basic shape of a rose window (**Documented**).
- **Rose windows came before the science.** The first true rose window is usually dated to the west front of Saint-Denis near Paris, about 1140, developing from the Roman *oculus* (a round opening) and the Romanesque "wheel window". Rose windows are laid out on radial divisions, often of 12, or of 4, 6 or 8. If the medieval builders knew about sound patterns, they knew about them more than 600 years before Chladni (**Documented** dates / **Speculative** link). See [the owner's position](#the-owners-position-on-the-600-year-gap) below.
- **The ancients did design for sound.** The Roman architect Vitruvius, writing in the 1st century BC, described bronze or clay "sounding vessels" (*echea*) placed in theatres to improve the sound. Clay "acoustic jars" have been found built into the walls of a number of medieval churches. So the idea that builders shaped buildings for sound is documented, though not for rose windows specifically (**Documented**).
- **Rosslyn Chapel.** Thomas and Stuart Mitchell claim that the 213 carved cubes in Rosslyn Chapel in Scotland show Chladni patterns, and turned them into music, the "Rosslyn Motet" (reported in 2005–07). It's the best-known attempt to link carvings to cymatics. It hasn't been accepted by historians, although one expert on early Scottish music said it was "by no means implausible" (**Reported**).
- **Ancient chambers that resonate.** Studies from the 1990s onwards reported that several Neolithic chambers, including the Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum in Malta, resonate strongly at around 95–120 Hz, often around 110 Hz, which is in the range of a low male voice. A small pilot study of 30 people (2008) reported changes in brain activity at 110 Hz. A later, more careful study of the Hypogeum (*Antiquity*, 2016) said earlier work lacked rigour (**Reported**).
- **Medieval cathedrals and their music shaped each other.** Measurements at Notre-Dame de Paris (before and after the 2019 fire) show a reverberation of several seconds, and research on its 12th-century choir suggests the long echo influenced how chant and early polyphony were sung there (**Documented**).
- **Organs and bells in this project.** The [Royal Albert Hall](places/uk/royal-albert-hall/royal-albert-hall.md) and [Alexandra Palace](places/uk/alexandra-palace/alexandra-palace.md) were built around huge Willis organs. [Manchester Town Hall](places/uk/manchester-town-hall/manchester-town-hall.md) has a great bell, "Great Abel", and the [Royal Courts of Justice](places/uk/royal-courts-of-justice/royal-courts-of-justice.md) has a clock tower with five bells and a rose window over its main entrance.
- **The Victorians didn't always get it right.** The Royal Albert Hall had such a bad echo that it wasn't fixed until 1969. On the owner's view, this could be a sign that the old knowledge of sound had been lost by the 19th century, and the revival buildings copied the forms without the understanding (**Speculative**).

### The owner's position on the 600-year gap

The owner believes one of two things must be true:

1. **They knew it 600 years before Chladni.** The medieval builders understood how sound shapes matter, and that knowledge was lost and only "rediscovered" by Chladni in 1787 and Jenny in 1967.
2. **The timeline is off.** If the [letters in dates](#letters-in-dates-the-j-argument) argument is right and history has been stretched, the rose windows may not be as old as their official dates say, and the gap between them and Chladni's experiments may be much shorter than it looks.

Either way, the owner's view is that the rose windows and the science of sound patterns belong to the same body of knowledge (**Speculative**).

How the two can be tested:

- **For "they knew it":** look for medieval or earlier writing that describes sound making patterns, or links windows, geometry and music. Medieval learning grouped music with geometry, arithmetic and astronomy (the *quadrivium*), and Pythagorean ideas about number and harmony were widely taught, so this is a reasonable place to look.
- **For "the timeline is off":** check how the dates are written in the records and inscriptions of the early rose-window churches, such as Saint-Denis and Chartres, for signs of the "J" or "I" pattern. Compare the official dates with independent dating: tree-ring dating of their roof timbers, radiocarbon dating of mortar, and the scientific dating of their glass. If the independent dates agree with the official ones, the gap is real; if they come out much later, that would support the owner's second reading.

### What critics say

- **Rose windows have other explanations.** They let light into the west and transept ends of a church, they grew out of earlier round windows, and they carried religious meaning: the wheel, the rose of the Virgin Mary, Christ in majesty at the centre. No medieval source found so far links them to sound.
- **Radial symmetry is common.** Anything laid out with a compass around a centre point (a wheel, a flower, a clock face, a rose window or a Chladni figure) will share the same basic shape. A match in general shape isn't evidence of a link; a match to the *specific* pattern of a *specific* note would be.
- **There are many Chladni patterns.** Each plate and each note gives a different figure, so with enough patterns to choose from, some will resemble almost any design.
- **Stained glass in lead isn't a sounding plate.** Window glass held in lead and stone tracery doesn't vibrate freely like a Chladni plate.
- **Big stone churches are poor for speech.** Their long echo suits slow chant and organ music, but makes preaching hard to hear, which is why pulpits with sounding boards were added later.

### How to test it

- **Catalogue the rose windows** in the project: date, number of divisions, rings and pattern. Compare each with the Chladni figures of a round plate (which are numbered by how many lines cross the centre and how many rings there are). If windows match particular figures much more closely than chance would allow, that would support the argument.
- **Look for medieval writing** on sound and building: Vitruvius, the sketchbook of the 13th-century architect Villard de Honnecourt (which includes drawings of rose windows), and builders' and musicians' treatises.
- **Compare measured acoustics with the organs and bells**: do the main resonances of a building match the pitch of its bells or the tuning of its organ?
- **Check for acoustic jars or vessels** in the walls and vaults of the older buildings in this project.
